# Ernest: Other Systems

How other systems treat nodes, the loss of one, and what passes between them: what readers found, set beside what [`mvp3.0.md`](mvp3.0.md) proposes. A finding is something to weigh. None is a decision.

Each reader was a research session of its own, which fetched the sources it names on 6 October 2026. A quotation is as the reader reported it, and was not fetched again for this document. Where a reader could not verify something, it is marked *unverified*. A finding is named by its reader's letter and a number: *O* for Orleans, *A* for Akka, *E* for the Erlang ecosystem, *T* for the typed and capability systems. Section 6's readers are *H*, *M* and *B*, and section 7's, read for [`code_update.md`](code_update.md), *U*, *V*, *S* and *D*.

## 1. What recurs

What more than one reader said, or what bears on a choice the proposal made. Each names the place in the proposal it bears on.

1. **Finding a failure takes us three to five times longer.** Akka finds one in seconds, Orleans in about 15 seconds, the proposal in 45 to 75, and as a constant. Akka's speed costs it false alarms, which its documentation admits (*A9*, *O7*). Bears on section 6, *The detector*, and section 7.
2. **A full queue does happen.** Akka's send queue overflows in practice under bursts, and Akka then drops the one message. The proposal ends every conversation between the two nodes (*A1*). Erlang's own answer, a sender made to wait, is one its documentation says "cannot be fixed", and its way out is memory without a bound (*E4*). Bears on section 5, point 4.
3. **One connection for everything.** Akka gives each peer three streams so that a large message cannot block an urgent one (*A2*). Partisan, Riak, RabbitMQ and gen_rpc each ended with several connections or channels to a peer (*E3*). Bears on section 5, point 5.
4. **A call's failure has two meanings.** A request that was never sent may be sent again, and one that was sent has an unknown outcome. The proposal answers `None` for both (*O1*). Bears on section 5, point 2.
5. **What must exist once needs something outside the nodes.** Each node decides alone, so two can disagree about a third; a lease works only through a store all of them consult, with a token that only grows (*O3*, *A5*). Bears on section 5, point 1.
6. **Finding a service matters more than spawning on a peer.** Akka's typed interface has no remote spawn at all and discourages it; a "find it again" helper is the first library anyone writes (*A4*, *O4*). The proposal now finds a service by a typed key that its node offers, as Akka, Swift and Gleam do, and ships no function for it. Bears on section 9, point 2, and section 10, the standing address.
7. **One side may be unable to dial.** Behind address translation or in containers, A reaches B and B does not reach A (*A3*). The proposal has one connection for both ways, opened by the side that can dial, and states what the other side loses after a loss (section 4, *An experiment*). Bears on section 5, point 13.
8. **Bytes prove the link and not the node.** A node that is starved and still connected looks alive (*O6*). Bears on section 6, *The detector*.
9. **A broken reference stays broken in the capability systems,** wherever it goes, and a new one is made from a durable reference (*T1*). The proposal first had an address die with its connection, and now has it outlive a loss, as Erlang, Akka and Swift have it at an ordinary loss. Bears on section 4, claim 7, and section 5, point 10.
10. **How an address is obtained again is a design of its own** in every system read: a durable reference in the capability systems, a typed key in Swift and Akka, a named service in Unison (*T3*). Bears on section 9, point 2.
11. **An address also lets its holder kill.** The capability systems give a reference the right to send and nothing more (*T6*). The proposal keeps Erlang's rule, that the holder can kill, and says so among its limits. Bears on section 5, point 12.
12. **What Erlang's own distribution cannot give.** Three things are decided inside the host's runtime, where neither a layer above nor another carrier beneath reaches them: that a message's type is checked before it reaches a mailbox, that a peer's data creates no atom, and that a peer's rights are narrower than everything (*E1*). The reader counted a fourth, an address that stays dead after a reconnection, which the proposal no longer asks. The proposal now rides on the distribution all the same: it keeps the first by a gateway of its own, and gives up the other two. Bears on section 9, point 1.
13. **A protocol of our own costs about what the host's does over TLS,** where frames are sent several at a time. Sent one at a time, small messages were 11 to 15 times slower in a reader's own measurement (*E2*). Bears on section 9, point 3.
14. **The runtime must not also be a node of distributed Erlang.** One that is hands everything to whoever holds its cookie (*E6*). The proposal is now one by choice, with TLS and listed keys before the cookie, and says that a peer is trusted completely.
15. **Erlang's distribution can carry frames of our own, with what hurts in it turned off.** Tried on OTP 29: TLS with listed keys and no port-mapper daemon, no mesh, a sender that does not wait, both nodes told of a loss, a pid that reaches its process again after one, and monitors and one-shot replies across nodes (section 4, *An experiment*). What stays is that a connected node may do anything. Bears on section 9, point 1.

## 2. Orleans and its relatives

Orleans is Microsoft's system of *virtual actors*: an actor is named by a logical identity, and the runtime decides on which machine it lives.

### How Orleans does it

- **Membership.** Machines probe each other and write their verdicts to a table outside the cluster. "Every silo is monitored by 10 other silos", "Probes are sent every 10 seconds", "3 missed probes trigger a suspicion", "2 suspicions are required to declare a silo dead", and "typical failure detection time is approximately 15 seconds" [O-1]. The agreement is borrowed: "this protocol outsources the hard problem of distributed consensus to the cloud" [O-1].
- **A machine declared dead is killed.** "Once a silo is declared dead in the table, everyone considers it dead, even if it isn't truly dead ... Once the silo learns it's dead (by reading its new status from the table), it terminates its process" [O-1].
- **Identity is logical.** A directory maps an actor's identity to a machine. When that machine dies, "you'll receive an exception ... The grain that failed with the silo will get automatically re-instantiated upon the next call to it" [O-4]. What the actor had not saved is gone: "Orleans does not impose a checkpointing strategy" [O-3].
- **One instance, eventually.** "In failure-free times, Orleans guarantees that an actor only has a single activation. However, when failures occur, this is only guaranteed eventually." [O-3]
- **Delivery.** "Orleans messaging delivery guarantees are at-most-once by default." [O-7] A request times out after 30 seconds by default [O-8]. With retries a message can arrive twice, and nothing removes the duplicate: "Orleans currently doesn't durably store which messages have already arrived ... (We believe this would be quite costly.)" [O-7]
- **Order.** Order for each pair of actors was built and then dropped: "The per-actor-pair state totals n² sequence numbers and queues. This is too much state to maintain efficiently." [O-3] Today "Message ordering was never guaranteed regardless of this attribute" [O-9].
- **Mixed builds.** Machines may run different builds, with numbered interfaces. "You should never change the signature of existing methods", and "Writing backward-compatible code can be hard and difficult to test" [O-13].
- **Placement** is automatic, since the indirection lets the runtime handle "actor placement and load balancing, deactivation of unused actors, and actor recovery after server failures, which are notoriously difficult for them to get right" [O-3].

### What it supports

- **`Unreachable`, and not dead.** Orleans kills a live machine to make its verdict true. The proposal says what is known.
- **Order for each sender and receiver.** Orleans dropped it for the state it cost for each pair of actors. The proposal's state is for each connection.
- **Telling the caller of a restart.** Orleans hides it, and the actor that returns lacks what it had not saved.
- **One build, for now.** Orleans' own guidelines show what mixed builds cost.
- **Agreement kept outside the core.** Orleans keeps it in a store outside too.
- **An address that names one process on one node.** The reader calls it practical and the older family. Orleans' authors state its cost: "As in Erlang, an actor's location is fixed at creation time, which prevents dynamic load balancing, actor migration, and automatic handling of machine failures" [O-3]. What people build on top is logical identity as a library.

### What it questions

- *O1.* **Split a call's failure.** "Never sent", which may be sent again, against "sent, outcome unknown".
- *O2.* **A convention for a request's identity,** since any second attempt after a reconnection can run twice.
- *O3.* **Two nodes can disagree about a third,** each deciding alone. A library for "exactly one" has to go through a store outside the nodes that orders every change, and the proposal should say so where it names a lease.
- *O4.* **A helper that reads the binding again after a loss** will be the first library, perhaps across a list of nodes. It is also where duplicates enter.
- *O5.* **Timers that survive a restart.** Orleans and both its relatives have them, since recovery needs something to wake the replacement.
- *O6.* **Bytes arriving find a dead link and not a starved node.** Orleans added checks of a node's own health because starved "slow nodes might otherwise incorrectly suspect healthy nodes" [O-1]. A time on a call covers a call; a monitor on a stuck node stays silent.
- *O7.* **Detection in about 15 seconds,** against the proposal's 45 to 75.

### Its relatives, and durable execution

- **Service Fabric's actors** live in parts of a replicated service, so the platform replicates their state. Its client tries again across failures, hence "Actors may receive duplicate messages from the same client" [O-18].
- **Dapr's actors** run beside the program, for any language, with a central service that decides placement. That this service uses Raft is *unverified*.
- **Temporal and Restate** record each step of a function and replay the record after a failure: "If your function crashes or fails, Restate replays the journal, skipping completed steps and resuming from exactly where it left off" [O-24]. The doubt survives at the edge, so "Temporal recommends that Activities be idempotent" [O-25]. Both need a central durable record, which the proposal declines. What carries over is smaller: a result that says "outcome unknown", and a request's identity as a convention.

### What the reader could not verify

The paper "Orleans: Cloud Computing for Everyone" was found and not read. Only two practitioners' accounts were found, [O-16] and [O-17], and no report of an incident. An older option for detecting deadlocks is *unverified*.

## 3. Akka and Pekko

Akka is the best-known actor system on the Java runtime, and Pekko is its open fork. The quotations are from Pekko's documentation, which the reader checked against Akka's for the central pages.

### How Akka does it

- **Remoting is not meant to be used alone.** "you would usually not use the Remoting concepts directly, but instead use the more high-level Pekko Cluster utilities" [A-1].
- **Three streams to each peer:** one for the system's own messages, one for ordinary messages, one for large ones. The first exists so that "a large user message cannot block an urgent system message" [A-1].
- **A program's messages** are sent at most once and "will not be received out-of-order" for each pair [A-4]. They are lost when a connection breaks, when "filling up the outbound send queue", and "if serialization or deserialization of a message fails (only that message will be dropped)" [A-1]. The queue holds 3072: "Messages will be dropped if the queue becomes full" [A-2]. A hole inside a stream is silent.
- **The system's own messages,** a watch and its notice among them, "are delivered ... with 'exactly-once' guarantee by confirming each message and resending unconfirmed messages" [A-1].
- **The detector** adapts its patience to the heartbeats it has seen, which come every second. It allows a pause of 3 seconds within a cluster and 10 seconds for a watch outside one [A-2][A-5][A-8].
- **Unreachable, down, removed.** Unreachable "is not a separate member state but rather a flag", and can be withdrawn. Down is decided by a rule or an operator. A node that was downed "cannot join the cluster again ... the process has to be restarted" [A-7].
- **Quarantine.** "It is the specific incarnation (UID) that is quarantined. The only way to recover from this state is to restart one of the actor systems." [A-1] The reason given is that the system's own messages "cannot be dropped because that would result in an inconsistent state between the systems" [A-1].
- **A watch within a cluster says nothing on unreachability alone.** Its notice comes "when the unreachable cluster node has been downed and removed" [A-11], and carries no reason.
- **Split brain.** "network partitions (split brain scenarios) and machine crashes are indistinguishable for the observer" [A-9]. A resolver decides which side lives, after 20 seconds of stability. It exists because downing by timeout means "two singleton instances or two sharded entities with the same identifier would be running" [A-9]. A pause defeats it: "When the node un-pauses there will be a short time before it sees its self as down where singletons and sharded actors are still running" [A-9].
- **Types across nodes.** A typed address crosses as a text, and the type is taken from whoever reads it back: nothing checks the message's type on arrival. This is read from the code [A-13].
- **A spawn on another node** "isn't supported in Typed. This feature would be discouraged because it often results in tight coupling between nodes and undesirable failure handling." [A-17]
- **Finding a service** is by a key that is replicated: "each node will eventually reach the same set of actors per ServiceKey" [A-15].

### What goes wrong in production

- **Quarantine removed nodes behind the resolver's back** and caused a split brain, in 2018. The Akka team's answer: "The decision of downing should be completely in control of the downing provider" [A-19][A-20].
- **The send queue overflows under bursts.** The team's answer names sustained overload and "head of line blocking, if a few of the messages are very large" [A-21].
- **False unreachability** from "long (unexpected) garbage collection pauses, overloading the system, too restrictive CPU quotas" [A-5][A-22].
- **A killed node stays a member** and stalls the membership until it is downed [A-7].
- **Address translation and containers** break the rule that "if a system A can connect to a system B then system B must also be able to connect to system A independently" [A-24].

### What it supports

- **No membership and no agreement in the core.** The resolver, quarantine against downing, and a stalled membership all come from membership that nodes share.
- **A loss that ends addresses and not the node.** It has quarantine's consistency without the restart.
- **At most once.** "The only meaningful way for a sender to know whether an interaction was successful is by receiving a business-level acknowledgement message" [A-4].
- **A long fixed timeout with any bytes as a sign of life.** Akka's separate stream was built for "reducing false failure detection in case of heavy traffic" [A-1].
- **One build.** Nodes of one service "share the same code and are deployed together" [A-25].
- **The type checked on arrival, and a call that learns of its callee's death.** Akka has neither. Its `ask` ends by timeout alone: "the receiving actor does not know and may still process it to completion" [A-16].
- **No gap.** Akka keeps that for the system's own messages alone.

### What it questions

- *A1.* **A full queue as the loss of everything.** Akka keeps that for the system's own messages, and a program's bursts overflow in practice [A-21]. One fast producer would end every address and monitor between two nodes. The reader suggests a limit in bytes, a way to see how much waits, and a library in which "The flow control is driven by the consumer side" [A-26].
- *A2.* **One connection.** A large message delays every call that has a time on it. Erlang keeps the order of a pair without separate streams: "Use fragmented distribution messages to send large messages" [A-27].
- *A3.* **Two nodes that dial at once, and a node that cannot be dialled.** Akka avoids both by connections that carry traffic one way. One connection for both ways needs a rule for the tie, which the proposal has, and a stated answer where only one side can dial, which it has not.
- *A4.* **A spawn on a peer.** Say what a spawned process does when its spawner's node is lost, and weigh a list for each peer of what it may start, as Akka's older interface has because a spawn "can potentially be abused" [A-18].
- *A5.* **A lease needs a token that only grows,** since "a lease can be lost due to a timeout with the third party system" [A-35], and a paused node acts on for a moment.
- *A6.* **A message that cannot be decoded.** Akka drops it, which the proposal's rule against a gap forbids. So a build's identity in the hello, and a refusal, are right for now; whether a type's hash must be exact or may allow a compatible type is a later question.
- *A7.* **A number drawn at each start,** in the hello and in every address. The proposal has it; the reader was not told so.
- *A8.* **The delay before a dial.** Akka's is a fixed second. The proposal's grew and had a random part when the reader read it; on Erlang's distribution it has none (*An experiment*, section 4).
- *A9.* **Detection in seconds,** against the proposal's 45 to 75, at the price of the false alarms above.

### What the reader could not verify

A post from Credit Karma on quarantine is often cited, and its site did not answer. Whether the fix to the incident of 2018 changed quarantine in later versions was not checked. One practitioner's page [A-23] was read through a summary and is not quoted.

## 4. The Erlang ecosystem

Erlang's own distribution, what has been built beside it, and the evidence for and against standing on it, which is unsolved point 1 of the proposal. The reader checked its quotations from Erlang's documentation, the Partisan paper and OTP's release notes against the text. A quotation marked † came through a tool that summarises, and its wording is to be checked before it is cited.

### What Erlang's distribution promises

- **Order for a pair, and silent loss.** "When communicating over the distribution, signals can be lost if the distribution channel goes down." And of two signals: "`S1` is guaranteed not to arrive after `S2`. Note that `S1` may or may not have been lost." [E-1]
- **A held pid is not dead after a loss.** The reader tried it on OTP 29: after a disconnection the monitor fired `noconnection`, and the next send to the same pid connected again and was delivered. Only a restart of the node changes the number that marks its pids [E-6].
- **Connecting again** is a setting for the whole node: never, or once, "If a node goes down, it must thereafter be explicitly connected." [E-3]
- **The mesh.** "Connections are by default transitive." [E-2] Since OTP 25 the module `global` prevents overlapping partitions "by actively disconnecting from nodes that reports that they have lost connections to other nodes" [E-4].

### Where it hurts

- **One connection for a pair.** "a large message can thus hold heartbeats back" [E-19]. OTP 22 sends a large message in fragments; it is still one stream.
- **A sender made to wait.** "the sending process may be suspended even though the signal is supposed to be sent asynchronously", which "cannot be fixed" [E-1]. A flag turns the wait off, and without a limit of its own that "will typically cause the sending runtime system to crash on an out of memory condition" [E-8].
- **Size.** The Partisan paper cites 200 nodes at Ericsson and Riak "not … beyond 60" [E-17].
- **Atoms.** A peer's data can "create resources, such as atoms and remote references, that cannot be garbage collected" [E-8], and distribution has no safe decoding.
- **Trust.** "The Erlang Distribution protocol is not by itself secure and does not aim to be so." [E-6] "if one node in a cluster is compromised, all nodes are" [E-20]. TLS proves who a peer is and narrows nothing it may do.

### What was built beside it

- **Partisan** replaces the distribution with a library of its own, and reports "up to an order of magnitude increase in the number of nodes" and "up to a 38.07x increase in throughput" [E-17]. Its criticism is the one connection: "a single TCP connection, therefore multiplexing actor-to-actor communication on a single channel" [E-17]. It had to write its own versions of Erlang's servers and of monitoring†.
- **Another carrier.** Erlang lets a module replace how nodes are found, connected and authenticated [E-5]. It must deliver bytes "in the exact same order, with no loss", and what a signal means, whether a pid is valid, how data is decoded and what a peer may do all stay the runtime's.
- **Languages.** The reader found none on the Erlang runtime that avoids Erlang's distribution.
- **Large deployments** work around it. Riak "avoids using Distributed Erlang for background data synchronization … to avoid head-of-line blocking" [E-17]. RabbitMQ moved its metadata to Raft, since the earlier store's "weakness is its failure recovery characteristics, in particular when it comes to network partitions"† [E-26]. Discord groups its sends by node†, and WhatsApp runs a filter on the distribution's messages [E-36].

### The three ways

| Way | What it gives | What it cannot give | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| A protocol of our own over TLS, as proposed | All that is asked, by construction: identity by key, addresses bound to a connection, a decoder of our own, one typed way in, any shape of network | Erlang's sends, links, monitors and tools across nodes; heartbeats, limits and fragments are ours to write | About the host's own over TLS, where frames are sent several at a time |
| Erlang's distribution, with our rules above it | Links, monitors, spawns and tools; no mesh, by a setting; identity by key in part | A held pid lives again; nothing checks a message before the mailbox; a peer creates atoms; any peer can end, start and call anything; the cookie remains | The fastest without encryption |
| Another carrier beneath Erlang's distribution | Transport, authentication and discovery of our own; several streams | The same as the second way for pids, checks, atoms and trust | About the host's own over TLS |

### A measurement

The reader measured on one machine, both nodes on it, on OTP 29, one to three runs each, with no limit on the sender in the protocol of our own. It is a first look and no more.

| Transport | A round trip | 100 bytes, 16 senders | 10 KB | 1 MB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Erlang's, no encryption | 73 to 102 µs | 82 to 94 thousand a second | 460 to 570 MB/s | 1280 to 1470 MB/s |
| Erlang's over TLS | 153 µs | 194 thousand a second | 279 MB/s | 333 MB/s |
| Our own over TCP, a message a frame | 84 to 98 µs | 29 thousand a second | 190 to 213 MB/s | 671 MB/s |
| Our own over TCP, up to 32 a frame | 88 µs | 324 thousand a second | 539 MB/s | 558 MB/s |
| Our own over mutual TLS 1.3, a message a frame | 144 to 168 µs | 15 thousand a second | 114 to 116 MB/s | 330 to 346 MB/s |
| Our own over mutual TLS 1.3, up to 32 a frame | 151 µs | 225 thousand a second | 253 MB/s | 241 MB/s |

Over TLS the two are within about a third of each other. Sending several messages in a frame decides the rate of small messages. Without encryption Erlang's is about twice as fast for large values. The reader found no published comparison of the two.

### An experiment

After the reader's report, the carrier was tried: four nodes on one machine, on OTP 29, each with a self-signed certificate and a list of the keys it accepts. Its code and how to run it are in `proposals/nodes_and_code/experiments/erlang_distribution/`.

| Tried | Found |
|---|---|
| TLS between nodes that list each other, with no port-mapper daemon | It connects. A table from a node's name to its port stands in for the daemon |
| A node that is not listed | Refused in the TLS handshake, by a rule of ours that accepts a peer by its public key |
| A certificate that names another host, and one that expired | Accepted by its key all the same, once the rule is told to answer the host's `cert_expired` as it answers `selfsigned_peer`; with the rule as the host has it an expired certificate is refused. A node's name on the carrier need not hold its address either: a name that resolves nowhere works, the table answering for it |
| No mesh | With a setting, two nodes that share a peer stay unconnected. A connection opens at the first send |
| A sender that does not wait | With the peer stopped, a send that refuses to wait is refused at once; the node then ends the connection itself, and both nodes are told | The proposal keeps the host's own way instead, a sender that waits, which the experiment's first step 8 run showed: a thousand sends behind a dial that nothing answered held the sender until the dial was given up |
| A silent peer, with the timeout at 4 seconds | Found lost after 4.6 to 4.8 seconds. A monitor on its process gives `noconnection`. The peer is told as it wakes |
| An address after a loss | The same pid reaches the same process, and the connection opens again by itself |
| Monitors and replies | A kill and a fault arrive with Ernest's own reasons. A reply through an alias takes one answer and drops a second |
| A node started again | A monitor on a process of its earlier start gives `noproc`, and a send to it is dropped |
| Two nodes with one key and name | To their peer they are one node. Each one's dial ends the other's connection, with `wait_pending` at the peer; what is sent to the other's processes is dropped, and a monitor on them gives `noconnection` |
| A node with another cookie | Refused in the host's handshake, with nothing sent and the other connections untouched. So the cookie can carry the build's fingerprint, and a node of another build is refused before anything passes |
| A parted network, through a proxy that drops bytes and closes | Both ways: both nodes find the loss within the detector's time. One way: the side that hears nothing finds it within the detector's time, and its close passes the open direction, so the other side is told at once |
| A node that ends its processes and stops | A halt, with or without a disconnect first, loses the `Down`s still in the buffer: the peer's monitor gives `noconnection`. The host's orderly stop lets them cross: the monitor gives the process's own reason |
| A send where no connection is open | It returns in microseconds, whether the peer's port refuses or nothing answers there. A refused dial costs about 90 microseconds, and the host makes one at each send, with no delay between them. Where nothing answers, the sends wait behind one dial, which the host gives up after 7 seconds |
| A node that does not listen, which its peer has no address for | The peer cannot connect to it. Once it has dialled, the peer reaches it and spawns on it over that connection. After a loss both are told, and what the peer sends is dropped until the node dials again; the same pid then reaches the same process. The host calls such a node hidden: it is left out of `nodes/0`, and of the notices of a loss unless every kind of node is asked for |

So the host gives the handshake, the detector, a loss that both nodes run, an address that outlives a loss, monitors and one-shot replies. What would be ours is a frame that carries a message's type, the check before a mailbox, and the check that two nodes run one program. Three things it does not show: a network that really parts, a certificate that names another host, and any speed. And one thing nothing turns off: a connected node may start, end and call anything on the other.

### What it questions, and advises

- *E1.* **Stay with a protocol of our own.** What the other two ways cannot give is decided in the runtime, and no carrier or layer reaches it. The price is to build failure detection, monitors and limits ourselves.
- *E2.* **Send several frames at a time** from the connection's process, and keep the heartbeat out of the queue of data.
- *E3.* **Do not copy the single stream.** Leave room in a frame for fragments and for more than one connection to a peer.
- *E4.* **Say what a sender sees when the queue is full.** Erlang's waiting sender cannot be fixed, and its way out has no bound.
- *E5.* **Bind an address to its connection.** Erlang needed aliases, thirty years on, to come near it.
- *E6.* **Run the host without its distribution,** or with one that listens to this machine alone. A node that is also a node of distributed Erlang gives everything to whoever holds the cookie. Trust a peer's key and not an authority that signs keys.

### What the reader could not verify

The versions that introduced two settings. The figure of 1,500 nodes in one cluster at WhatsApp, which is the text of a search result for a talk. Why Erlang's own distribution was slower than the protocol of our own for small messages in the measurement.

## 5. Typed and capability systems

Swift's distributed actors, Unison and its cloud, and the capability systems that descend from the language E: OCapN, its protocol CapTP, and Spritely Goblins. The reader took the quotations for Swift, Unison's `Remote`, OCapN, Goblins, E and Cloud Haskell from the sources themselves. Those for Ray, Gleam, wasmCloud and Cap'n Proto came through a tool that summarises, and their wording is to be checked before it is cited.

### Swift's distributed actors

- **A remote call is typed differently, by the actor's kind and not by where it is.** A call from outside a distributed actor can always fail and always waits, even where the actor is local: "It is, by design, not possible to *statically* determine if a distributed actor instance is remote or local, therefore all programming against a distributed actor must be done as-if it was remote." [T-1] The benefit argued: "it allows us to surface any potential network issues that might occur during these calls" [T-1].
- **What crosses** is checked when the program is compiled, and a function is refused [T-1]. The target on the wire is the method's full signature: "any change in the method signature will result in not being able to resolve the target method anymore" [T-2]. No code moves.
- **Failure.** A membership protocol with gossip finds failures. On a node's fall, "'terminated' signals are generated for all actors watching" [T-4], "regardless if it really has deinitialized or not" [T-3]. A fall is for ever: "once a node is down/dead, it may never again be considered up/alive" [T-5]. A lost connection alone ends nothing.
- **Delivery.** A call has a time of 5 seconds by default. "A remote call may be delivered (and processed!) successfully, while only the reply to it may not" [T-7].
- **Finding** is by a typed key that an actor registers under [T-8].

### Unison and its cloud

- **Documented.** "Each Unison definition is identified by a hash of its syntax tree", and whoever receives a computation "requests the ones it's missing and the sender syncs them on the fly" [T-12]. Any value can be sent, a function among them.
- **Failure is in the type.** Distribution is an ability, `Remote`, and its operations answer `Either Failure` [T-14]. A service is typed by what it takes and what it gives [T-15].
- **Not documented, as far as the reader found:** what is promised of delivery and of order, what a node's death does to what runs on it, and whether a type is checked on arrival. The cloud's runtime is a licensed product and not open [T-16]. "As easy as a local function call" [T-13] is what it is sold with, and the ability in the type says otherwise.

### OCapN, Goblins and E

- **A loss is for ever, as in the proposal.** "Even after a partition heals, all references broken by that partition stay broken." "A partition simultaneously breaks all references crossing in a given direction between two vats." [T-22]
- **Order without a gap.** "Later messages will only be delivered by a reference if all earlier messages sent on that same reference were already delivered" [T-22]. OCapN requires "only one active session between two peers" [T-18].
- **The difference in the language** is between a call that waits and a send that does not, and it follows the boundary between two units of computation, not between two machines [T-21][T-22]. A failure is a broken promise.
- **Getting a reference again.** A durable reference holds the key's fingerprint, hints of where the node is, and a secret number. It "can be passed between vats even when the vat of the target object is inaccessible", and "one makes a new reference from an offline capability" [T-22].
- **A reference handed over by a third party.** The giver deposits the gift with the node that owns the object and sends the receiver a signed note; the receiver opens a session of its own and collects it [T-18]. The specification does not say what follows where the giver's session ends before the gift is collected, and OCapN is "still pre-specification" [T-20].
- **A broken reference stays broken wherever it goes.** "Broken is a terminal state (once Broken always Broken)", and broken references "are transitively passed by copy" [T-23].

### Others, briefly

- **Cloud Haskell.** "Every node is running the same code", and only a function known when the program is compiled crosses [T-29]. "Once a network connection breaks (even temporarily) no further communication on that connection will be possible" until the program calls `reconnect`, accepting "that some messages to B might have been lost" [T-29].
- **Ray.** "By default, actor tasks execute with at-most-once semantics", and a second attempt is asked for [T-25].
- **Proto.Actor.** "At-most-once delivery" and "message ordering per sender–receiver pair" [T-26].
- **Gleam.** "Type safe message passing is implemented in Gleam in libraries" [T-31]. The reader found no stated position on distribution.
- **Pony** has distribution in a thesis of 2013 alone. The reader found no stated reason.

### What it supports

- **A loss that is for ever, and getting an address again,** is E's design, argued from consistency. Cloud Haskell and Akka came to nearby positions.
- **At most once, order for each pair, nothing tried again unseen** is the default in Proto.Actor, Ray, Swift and Akka.
- **A time on a call, and "no answer",** match Swift's own admission that a lost answer and a lost call look the same.
- **No function in a message, and a spawn by reference within one build,** is Cloud Haskell's line and Swift's. Unison shows that the later step, code by its hash, works.
- **The same operations on one node and across nodes.** Swift types a remote call differently because a local call there cannot otherwise fail. A call in Ernest already answers "no answer" on one node, so it stands where E stands and needs nothing more.
- **No membership** is workable because a loss is a connection's. Swift and Akka need gossip, a verdict and a record of the fallen to make "terminated" final.

### What it questions

- *T1.* **Is being dead said of the value or of its holder?** Where A sends its dead address to C and C sends it back, is it live? Does it equal a fresh one? E answers by making breakage a reference's own: a broken reference is passed on as broken, and a third party introduces only by a live reference of its own.
- *T2.* **Both sides must come to know of the loss.** E relies on "eventual common knowledge of the loss of connection" [T-22], and OCapN states one session for each pair with a rule for crossed hellos. The proposal has both.
- *T3.* **How an address is obtained again.** Every system has an answer of its own: a durable reference, a first object every session starts from, a typed key, a named service. The proposal needs a typed and lasting form that keeps an address a permission.
- *T4.* **A type's hash and a new version.** Swift met this with signatures, and Unison puts a name between a service and its hash. A changed message type cuts nodes of two versions off from each other.
- *T5.* **Order across an introduction.** A's message to X, and the message B sends X after A introduced them, can arrive in either order. E strengthened its order for this. The proposal should state its rule.
- *T6.* **The right to kill travels with the right to send.** A reference in OCapN conveys messaging alone. An address that only sends would need a process in front of it.

## 6. Code by its hash

Read on 7 October 2026 by three readers, for [`mvp3.1.md`](mvp3.1.md), after its first question was decided: the hash over the canonical typed tree, references by hash, no own name and no positions, types nominal with their members, a group as one hash with positions, no project name. What each found is set beside that decision. The second and third readers' findings follow when they report.

### How Unison, Dhall and Nix hash definitions

The reader read Unison's hashing code (`unison-hashing-v2`), its codebase migrations, its issue 2787 and pull request 6007; Dhall's standard and changelog; Nix's RFC 62 and manual.

- **Local names and the definition's own name are erased** (*H1*). "All named arguments are replaced by positionally-numbered variable references"; names are "separately stored metadata that don't affect the function's hash". Confirms.
- **A reference is the bare hash of what it names** (*H2*). `x = 1 + 1` and `y = x` hash the same, which Unison calls transparency. Confirms, and asks that the choice be made on purpose.
- **Unison's order within a cycle is defective, and has been since 2022** (*H3*). The docs say a cycle's members are sorted by their hashes with the cycle removed; members that differ only in which other member they name tie, the tie falls back to names, and "equivalent cycles [get] different hashes, and possibly different cycles [get] the same hash". A 2025 change makes hashing fail on a tie and asks the user to add a dummy definition. Corrects: a group's position must be defined, by source order or by an order that rejects ties, and the group is the strongly connected component.
- **A term's hash includes its inferred type, and effects are hashed as a sorted set** (*H4*). Confirms typed hashing; adds that an effect row needs a canonical order, and that hashing an inferred type ties every hash to the inference.
- **Unison hashes a type by its structure, with every identifier removed, and gives a unique type a minted identity that a rename keeps** (*H5*). Constructor and field names are not hashed. Corrects: a nominal hash means a type rename is a new type, and every dependent changes; either say so or mint an identity once.
- **Types and terms are hashed in separate passes with a kind tag each, and a cycle never spans a type and a term** (*H6*). Corrects: a `compare` whose tree names its type, inside that type's hash, is a type-to-term cycle; the members go beside the type's hash, not in it.
- **The hashing version is mixed into every hash, and a change is a complete migration** (*H7*). Adds: the scheme's version is prefixed to every hash so that hashes of two schemes are never equal.
- **Unison's rehash migration kept old hashes and two codebases mapped one old hash to two new objects, which crashed syncing** (*H8*). Adds: a migration is total and one-way.
- **Dhall hashes the binary encoding of the alpha-beta-normal form, bound variables renamed to one name with an index** (*H9*). Confirms the erasure; contrasts in inlining dependencies rather than referencing them.
- **Dhall once put the language version into the hash and removed it, since every release changed every hash** (*H10*). Corrects a possible reading: the version in the hash is the scheme's own, never the toolchain's.
- **Every change to Dhall's normaliser changed hashes** (*H11*). Adds: whatever runs before hashing is part of the frozen form, desugaring, elaboration, inferred types, how supplies are filled.
- **Dhall fixes the encoding of literals, floats, zero's sign, big integers, text, and sorts fields, so that hashes agree across platforms** (*H12*). Adds: the canonical form fixes its literals and sorts explicitly, never by a map's iteration order, with a self-describing prefix.
- **Nix hashes a reference by what it names, so that an irrelevant change does not spread** (*H13*). Confirms.
- **Changing Nix's addressing took years, and content addressing needs determinism** (*H14*). Adds: freeze the form before code is shared.

What the reader would tell the designers first: define a group's position before anything else; a nominal type hash and members inside it conflict with rename-invariance and make a cycle; version the scheme on its own, mixed into every hash, and plan the one-way rehash now.

### How Unison, Git, Nix, IPFS and Cloud Haskell move code by hash

The reader read Unison's runtime and sync code, the Big Idea page and the JIT announcement; Git's pack protocol and `receive-pack`; Nix's `copyPaths` and its manual; IPFS's Bitswap; the Cloud Haskell paper and GHC's static pointers; OTP's `code` module. For the proposal's exchange at a spawn: the sender lists the hashes the function references, the peer answers with those it lacks, the sender ships them, the peer verifies, compiles and loads the closure at once, and only then starts the process.

- **Unison ships the root's code first and fetches what is missing** (*M1*). "The sender ships the bytecode tree to the recipient, who inspects the bytecode for any hashes it's missing. If it already has all the hashes, it can run the computation; otherwise, it requests the ones it's missing and the sender syncs them on the fly. They'll be cached for next time." Confirms the exchange; the order differs.
- **Unison ships a checked intermediate form, not source, and compiles it on arrival** (*M2*). The code "is serialized and sent from the client, and needs to be deserialized, compiled and executed on the server". Adds a third answer to what a code frame carries.
- **Unison's cache adds a batch all or nothing, and names what it lacks** (*M3*). Confirms atomic loading of the whole closure.
- **Unison hashes the shipped form again, by a second hash, and refuses a group whose members' hashes differ** (*M4*). Adds: a shipped compiled form needs a hash of its own or the sender's trust, and a group is checked as one unit.
- **Builtins are never shipped** (*M5*): "loaded code cannot be associated to a builtin link"; serialization and the sync protocol are versioned, with an error for an unsupported version. Adds: a foreign function the peer lacks is known before any code moves, and belongs in the first frame.
- **Unison's store never holds an entity whose dependencies are absent** (*M6*): a "temp entity" waits outside the store until its dependencies are there. Adds the invariant: a hash in the store has its whole closure in the store.
- **Unison's pull negotiates over coarse roots and streams dependencies first, validating in the same transaction** (*M7*); its push answers "need dependencies" and "entity hash mismatch". Confirms checking on arrival; adds that a known root stands for its subtree.
- **Git negotiates over commits, one commit standing for its history, and quarantines incoming objects until checked** (*M8*, *M9*). "Placed into a temporary 'quarantine' directory... and migrated into the main object store only after the pre-receive hook has completed"; and the warning that a failed fetch can leave "malicious objects" that a later fetch then trusts. Adds: nothing enters the cache before the closure is complete and verified.
- **Nix's `copyPaths` is the proposal's exchange** (*M10*): the sender computes the closure, asks the destination which paths are valid, and copies the missing ones dependencies first, after pinning what the destination said it has. Adds: the receiver pins what it answered "have" until the load is done.
- **Nix trusts a content-addressed object without a signature, and needs a signature for everything else** (*M12*): "Content-addressed paths are inherently trustworthy." Decides the frame question in principle: a compiled binary named by its source's hash is input-addressed and can only be trusted; a hashed form verifies itself.
- **Nix caches a "not found" answer for a limited time** (*M13*). Adds: a positive entry by hash never needs invalidation, a negative one expires.
- **IPFS exchanges want-lists of content ids** (*M14*). Confirms the shape; suggests one request per hash where several spawns wait on the same code.
- **Cloud Haskell ships a label for code, since every node runs one binary, and fingerprints a type with everything it depends on** (*M15*). Confirms the proposal's hashing of types with their dependencies, by contrast.
- **OTP's `code:atomic_load/1` loads all or none, atoms are never collected, and loaded modules are never unloaded by themselves** (*M16*). Adds the risk: every hash a module name grows the atom table without bound unless unreferenced modules are unloaded.

What the reader would tell the designers first: ship the hashed form and compile it on arrival, caching the compiled form by hash, unless peers are trusted; make "a hash in the store has its closure in the store" an invariant, with quarantine until verified, one atomic load, and the root sent first so that the common case costs one small exchange; settle foreign functions and memory before any code moves.

### The BEAM with many modules, measured

The reader read OTP's sources (`module.c`, `index.c`, `erl_fun.c`, `export.c`, `code.erl`, `code_server.erl`, `erts_code_purger.erl`, `beam_bif_load.c`) and measured on this machine, OTP 29, with generated modules of two functions and one fun; its scripts and raw output are under the session's scratch directory, not in the repository. Read against a module of the host for each definition, named by its hash, loaded by `atomic_load`, never changed, unloaded when nothing refers to it.

- **Atoms are never reclaimed; the default limit is 1,048,576** (*B1*). 10,000 modules loaded and purged left the atom count where loading had put it. Confirms, and is not the limit that binds.
- **A node can load at most 65,536 distinct module names in its life, and exits when the limit is reached** (*B2*). `MODULE_LIMIT (64*1024)`; the index table's only erase undoes an aborted staging; the node ends with "no more index entries in module_code (max=65536)" and a crash dump. Measured twice: 65,000 modules loaded in batches, and the same with every batch deleted and purged after loading, which crashed all the same at 65,158 entries. A flag to raise it, `+zmml`, is on OTP's master since August 2026 and not in OTP 29. Corrects: unloading gives no name back.
- **Since OTP 28 a fun-table entry lives for ever, one per fun per module version** (*B3*). "This required making fun entries live forever (like export entries)"; the limit is 524,288 and cannot be raised; the key holds the module's `uniq`, so every version adds entries. Measured: 1,411 to 11,411 on load, unchanged after purge. Confirms the leak for OTP 28 and later.
- **Export entries are never freed, two at least per module** (*B4*). Limit 524,288. Adds a second ceiling of about 262,000 modules.
- **`erlang:memory(code)` counts those tables: about 1.7 kB per module name, never reclaimed** (*B5*). Measured, 10,000 modules: 8.8 MB before, 38.0 MB loaded, 26.0 MB after purging all; with 65,000 loaded and purged in turn, 123 MB with 158 modules loaded.
- **Batch loading costs a tenth of loading one by one, and stays flat** (*B6*). 1,000 modules: 409 ms one by one, 47 ms by `prepare_loading` and `finish_loading`, 50 ms by `atomic_load`; 65,000 in batches of 1,000 at 41 to 75 ms a batch; `compile:forms` 1.46 ms a module. Confirms.
- **A purge is a synchronous call through the code server that signals every process and scans the whole fun table** (*B7*). 200 modules deleted and soft-purged: 5.8 ms each with no other process, 37 ms with 10,000, 320 ms with 100,000. Adds: purging cannot be batched and stalls all loading meanwhile.
- **The VM does not track funs: a purge ignores them, and a held closure fails with `undef` afterwards** (*B8*). "The check process code operation only checks for direct references to the code. Indirect references via funs will be ignored." Measured. Corrects: whether anything refers to a module is Ernest's own bookkeeping, or a closure in a value breaks.
- **`atomic_load` is all or nothing and fails with `not_purged` where old code of a module exists** (*B9*). Adds: skip a hash already loaded; no `-on_load`.
- **Only the final step of a load goes through the code server; preparing runs in the caller, in parallel** (*B10*). Confirms.
- **No unit of loaded code exists without a module atom** (*B11*). An interpreted fun runs 34 times slower; Elixir's compiler reuses a bounded pool of module names, since names cannot be reclaimed. Adds: a content hash as a name rules out reuse; fewer modules, or restarts, are the levers.
- **In interactive mode a call to a missing module goes to the code server's search of the code path** (*B12*). Adds: embedded mode, or hash modules off the code path.

What the reader would tell the designers first: one module for each definition version cannot run on a node that lives long, on OTP 29; make unloading rare, since each purge signals every process; the VM will not track references, so Ernest must, or not unload.

## 7. Code update

Read on 7 October 2026 by four readers, for [`code_update.md`](code_update.md), against its three questions: (a) whether a service written as a step function from state and message to state, the runtime holding state and mailbox, removes the handover, the drain and the forwarder; (b) whether `migrate` and the translation can be derived from a diff of two type declarations, leaving only the undeterminable to be written; (c) whether an upgrade can run as a generated test on every commit. The readers are *U* for the upgrade practice, OTP's appup and the proxies' hot restart; *V* for versioned actors, Orleans, Akka and their relatives; *S* for schema evolution and the step-function shape, Avro, protobuf, Elm, safecopy and Smalltalk; and *D* for dynamic software updating as a field, and CLOS. Each is a research session of its own, which fetched the sources it names; a quotation is as the reader reported it.

### What recurs

What more than one reader said, or what corrects a choice `code_update.md` made. Each names the place in that document it bears on.

1. **The upgrade as the recovery path is already how Orleans, Akka and Kitsune work, and all three lose the state in memory doing it.** Orleans deactivates the old activation and the new one reads storage (*V2*); Akka's sharded entity is stopped and restored from persistence (*V8*); Kitsune restarts the program from `main` with the old state migrated in (*D1*). The handover is Ernest's addition, not something the step function makes unnecessary. Bears on section 2, *An upgrade is a recovery the operator chooses the moment of*.
2. **The message boundary of a step function is the field's safe point, and con-freeness holds there by shape.** Every C and Java system put its update point at the head of the event loop, where no old frame is live (*D1*, *D2*); at hand-chosen loop heads no update failed where 15% failed at arbitrary points (*D13*); OTP's own in-place upgrade already is that shape, the runtime holding the mailbox, with no handover, drain or forwarder (*U3*), paying a suspend window instead (*U4*). Corrects section 2's frame, where the handover is the rule and in place the exception for P5: for a service whose state and mailbox the runtime holds, in place with no handover is the plain form; the handover is for the resource owner and for the node that stops. Bears on section 2, *In place is the exception*, and on the matrix.
3. **The step function removes the handover only where the runtime holds the resources as well as the state.** Elm's runtime can hold everything because an effect is a value the step returns and the runtime performs; a loop that opens a socket inside itself keeps the socket out of the runtime's hands (*S7*); Akka's runtime holds the mailbox and the program holds the state in a closure, so nothing can ask an actor for its state (*V8*); Akka Persistence refuses `become` for the actor whose state must survive, since a behaviour is state recovery cannot rebuild (*V9*). Bears on section 2, *What a replaceable service is*, and on section 7's second question.
4. **The derivable migration is one closed rule, the same in every system.** Match by name and type, a default for what is added, drop what is removed, promote a number's width, and an error for the rest: Avro's resolution (*S1*), Orleans's serializer (*V6*), Akka's Jackson rules (*V11*), Dapr's generator (*V13*), Ginseng, Rubah and CLOS (*D4*). What it is blind to: a rename, which reads as a removal and an addition and is answered by one annotation on the new type (*S2*, *D5*, *S5*), and a changed meaning behind an unchanged type (*D5*, *S6*). The derived form leaves a hole the checker refuses, never a default (*D4*). Bears on section 3's C2 and C3, and section 4.
5. **The way back is the new build's reverse `migrate`, shipped with it.** An old build cannot hold a function from a type written after it: safecopy's extensions (*S11*), Akka's two-deployment rollout (*V11*), Dapr's "no working path" (*V13*). Corrects section 2, *The way back*.
6. **The rollout's order is free one way only.** The successor serving both protocols is Confluent's FULL compatibility (*S3*); the reverse meeting, a new client and a service still on old code, is served by no translation, and Orleans answers it by deactivating the old activation on the first new call (*V4*); a protocol that is a conversation has no safe point inside a session (*D12*). Bears on section 4, *The order of a rollout is free*, and on the guide's rule: small, own protocols of independent requests.
7. **A stored state migrates at the read, and a `migrate` that is pure pays nothing for being lazy.** Every system read runs the adapter at the read and keeps the old form until the next write (*V12*, *V13*, *D9*, *U10*); every cost of laziness falls on an impure or non-idempotent transformer (*D8*), which Ernest's effect row rules out. Answers section 7's question on when `migrate` runs: at the read for a keeper's or a table's state, once and eagerly for a loop's.
8. **No system runs the upgrade as a test; the harness is derivable and the oracle is not.** Orleans, Durable Functions and Dapr test the shape at build and leave the run to advice (*V14*); HotCI built the loop by hand and skips itself where a restart is declared (*U11*); the identity upgrade, a version to itself, needs no oracle and checks the handover (*D14*); the systematic method applies the update at each boundary a test reaches, with an oracle that may be either version's (*D13*); Elm's debugger replays a recorded message log through the new `update` (*S9*); a committed baseline of hashes runs the shape half on every commit without starting anything (*V13*). Bears on section 7's last question.
9. **A connection is never handed over; a listening socket is.** nginx, HAProxy and Envoy pass the listening sockets and drain every connection to its own end (*U12*); a drain is minutes long, graded, and ends by force (*U13*). Corrects section 2, *The singleton*: a connection to a database is a P5, on the client side as on the server's.
10. **What the field paid for representation consistency, Ernest pays as a translation at every boundary between versions.** Ginseng and Mutatis rejected coexisting versions of a type as disastrous with side effects (*D3*); Ernest's two types for two versions is that rejected alternative, sound only because values are immutable and messages are copied, and its price is the drain and the second key. Bears on section 1, *Where Ernest starts*.
11. **The hand-written part is a few lines per changed type, and the retrofit is where the lines went.** Kitsune, Rubah and Ginseng measured it (*D6*); OTP's appup's hand-written order is between callers and callees of one build, which one atomic load removes (*U1*); what survives in practice is the C1 fix loaded behind an unchanged state and protocol (*U9*). Bears on section 3, *What the program writes*, and on the experiments' measure.
12. **A claim made during a deploy is weaker than the document's.** OTP's point of no return restarts the whole node into the old release (*U5*); the document does not yet say what becomes of the services already replaced when a later one refuses. Bears on section 4, *What is refused, and when*.

### The upgrade practice: OTP's appup and relup, Elixir's refusal, and the proxies' hot restart

The reader read OTP 29's *Release Handling* and *Appup Cookbook* chapters, the `appup` and `release_handler` references, the `sys` and `gen_server` pages, and *Upgrade when Erlang/OTP has Changed*; `Mix.Tasks.Release`'s "Hot Code Upgrades" section and Elixir issue 8612; Castle's and Forecastle's READMEs and FlyDeploy's wiki; Fred Hebert's *My favorite Erlang container* and the 2024 UCLouvain thesis *HotCI* built on it; *Learn You Some Erlang*'s relup chapter, Bob Ippolito's 2014 erlang-questions message, an Elixir forum thread on continuous deployment by hot upgrade, and HighScalability's summary of Rick Reed's 2014 WhatsApp talk; nginx's control page, Envoy's hot restart, draining and CLI pages, HAProxy's management manual and its 2017 seamless-reload post.

- **Everything OTP derives, it derives from an ordering the author wrote by hand** (*U1*). The appup's `update` carries `DepMods`, and "instructions for suspending processes using Mod come before instructions for suspending processes using modules in DepMods when upgrading, and conversely when downgrading" (appup reference); `systools:make_relup` then turns the appups "into a single list of low-level instructions in the right order" (Release Handling). The cookbook's reason: without them "a runtime error could occur during release upgrade if the new version of m1 is loaded first and calls ch3:available/0 before the new version of ch3 is loaded". Confirms section 1's reading of what is by hand and section 4's claim that the order is what to derive. Adds: the order OTP needs is between callers and callees of one build, not between services, so a build loaded in one act (section 6's *B9*, `atomic_load`) removes `DepMods` before any protocol question arises. Bears on (b).
- **`code_change` is untyped, optional, and runs only when the appup says `{advanced, Extra}`** (*U2*). `code_change(OldVsn | {down, Vsn}, State, Extra) -> {ok, NewState} | {error, Reason}`; "if a release upgrade/downgrade with Change = {advanced, Extra} specified in the .appup file is made when Module:code_change/3 is not implemented, the callback call will crash with an undef error reason" (gen_server); and a `ModType` flag, `static | dynamic`, decides whether the new code is loaded before or after the call. Confirms section 1's "a second path, hand-written, untyped". Adds: nothing checks the appup against the callback, which is exactly what the plan's refusal "State changed and no migrate" replaces. Bears on (b).
- **OTP's in-place upgrade already is a step function with the runtime holding the mailbox, and it hands over, drains and forwards nothing** (*U3*). "All those processes are suspended using sys:suspend, the new module version is loaded, and then the processes are resumed using sys:resume" (appup reference); "When the process is suspended, it only responds to other system messages, but not other messages" (sys), so ordinary messages wait in the mailbox and the pid is kept. Corrects section 2's frame, where the handover is the rule and in place the exception for P5 alone: for a service whose state and mailbox the runtime holds, OTP's shape answers (a) with no drain and no forwarder, and section 7's question about §6.4's order across the drain does not arise, since the mailbox is the same one. The drain and the forwarder exist in the document only because the successor has a new address. Bears on (a).
- **What in place costs instead is the suspend window, and OTP documents it** (*U4*). "During release handling, non-affected processes continue normal execution. This can lead to time-outs or other problems. For example, new processes created in the time window between suspending processes using a certain module, and loading a new version of this module, can execute old code" (Release Handling); the suspend time-out "is 5000 ms, unless otherwise specified" (sys), and "If a process does not respond, it is ignored. This can cause the process to die, either because it crashes when it spontaneously switches to new code, or as a result of a purge operation" (appup reference). Adds to section 4's "what only the running system decides": a step function removes the drain but not the quiescent point; a caller that times out against a suspended service is the cost in place carries and the handover does not, and the document should weigh the two against each other rather than bound one to P5. Bears on (a).
- **OTP's point of no return is a reboot into the old release** (*U5*). "If a crash occurs after this instruction, the system cannot recover and is restarted from the old release version" (appup reference); `load_object_code` "is to be placed first in the script to read all new code from the file to make the suspend-load-resume cycle less time-consuming"; "If a recoverable error occurs, the function returns {error,Reason} and the original application specifications are restored. If a non-recoverable error occurs, the system is restarted" (release_handler). Confirms section 4's shape, refuse before and answer during. Corrects section 2's way back: OTP's answer during a deploy is the whole node, so "the plan is stopped there with the service as it was" is a stronger promise than OTP makes, and the document does not yet say what becomes of the services already replaced when a later one refuses. Bears on (a), (c).
- **Even OTP's own applications mostly restart** (*U6*). "As of Erlang/OTP 17, most applications deliver a valid application upgrade file (appup)"; "Many of the applications use the restart_application instruction. These are applications for which it is not crucial to support real soft upgrade, for example, tools and library applications"; "The core applications ERTS, Kernel, STDLIB, and SASL never allow real soft upgrade" (*Upgrade when Erlang/OTP has Changed*). Confirms the matrix's C7 and section 3's default that a service which writes nothing is restarted. Bears on (c).
- **Elixir left hot upgrades out on grounds of practice, not mechanism** (*U7*). "The reason we don't provide hot code upgrades is because they are very complicated to perform in practice, as they require careful coding of your processes and applications as well as extensive testing"; "Given most teams can use other techniques that are language agnostic to upgrade their systems, such as Blue/Green deployments, Canary deployments, Rolling deployments, and others, hot upgrades are rarely a viable option" (Mix.Tasks.Release). Issue 8612 planned them as the third milestone, "then finally work on hot code upgrades with relups and appups", and that milestone was never built. Confirms section 1. Adds for (c): "extensive testing" is the cost Elixir judged nobody would pay; the test on every commit is the document's answer to it and must therefore cost the program nothing to write. Bears on (c).
- **What the Elixir libraries kept is OTP's `release_handler` with the appup still by hand, drafted for review at best** (*U8*). Castle: "Write an appup for each owned application that must be upgraded in place"; `mix castle.appup.gen` can "draft missing appup entries for review"; "Castle does not support the two-stage restart_new_emulator transition". FlyDeploy: "Hot upgrades are forward-only. Once new code is loaded into the BEAM VM, FlyDeploy cannot roll it back", and it cannot "Change supervision tree structure or process hierarchy". Adds: a generator whose output a human reviews is the field's state of the art for (b); and the supervisor row, C6, is where every tool read stops, which weighs for the document's choice to treat a supervisor as a P3 with a `migrate`. Bears on (b).
- **The field loads modules by script and skips relup** (*U9*). "most people never bothered, and the vast majority of live code upgrades are done by people writing manual scripts to load specific modules" (Hebert). "if you can avoid the whole procedure ... and do simple rolling upgrades by restarting VMs and booting new applications, I would recommend you do so", and "It is said that divisions of Ericsson that do use relups spend as much time testing them as they do testing their applications themselves" (LYSE). WhatsApp, as HighScalability summarised Rick Reed's 2014 talk: "Sometimes if it's just a hot patch it can be rolled out without a complete restart. This is rare. Usually upgrade the whole thing." Mochi: "We used hot code reloading a lot ... The trouble is when you change too much about the messages or data types flowing through the system you will have to write a fair amount of code" (Ippolito, 2014). Against that, an Elixir forum thread has two shops deploying by hot upgrade routinely through edeliver and Distillery, "works flawlessly". Confirms section 1's "the capability is real and the practice is not". Adds: what survives in practice is C1, a module loaded behind an unchanged state and protocol, so the C1 row is the one that must cost nothing and run with no plan at all. Bears on (b), (c).
- **Smoothver names the matrix's rows as a version number, and the field's trick makes a migration lazy** (*U10*). "RESTART version when you make a change that requires the server to be rebooted. RELUP version when you make a change that requires pausing workers and migrating state. RELOAD version when you make a change that requires reloading modules with no other transformation"; and "turn RELUP changes (which would require calling code change instructions) into basic code reloads by pattern matching an old structure and converting it on each call" (Hebert). Adds: Hebert's three numbers are C7, C2 and C1 declared by hand, which `ern diff` computes; and section 7's question whether `migrate` runs at the deploy or when the state is next read has a field answer, on touch, in the new code, since that is what turns an upgrade into a reload. Bears on (b).
- **An upgrade run as a CI test has been built, and it skips itself when a restart is declared** (*U11*). HotCI "involves launching an Erlang/OTP release, using a separate Erlang process to interact with and modify its state, executing the upgrade, verifying that the state is correct, downgrading the release and asserting that the final state is as expected"; its pipeline first asks "Does the release require a restart?" and if so writes "that the checks are skipped"; and the feature "is often underutilized because of the perceived complexities in comprehension, implementation, and testing" (Zenon, 2024). Confirms that (c) is feasible. Adds what section 7's last question does not list: the test needs a previous tagged build to exist, a state it can write before and read after, and the downgrade as half of the test, so the reverse `migrate` is tested on every commit too. Bears on (c).
- **The operating-system precedents hand over the listening socket and never the connection** (*U12*). nginx: "Old worker processes close listen sockets and continue to service old clients. After all clients are serviced, old worker processes are shut down." HAProxy `-x`: "connect to the specified socket and try to retrieve any listening sockets from the old process, and use them instead of trying to bind new ones", after which a finished process "continue[s] to process existing connections until they close. Once the last connection is closed, the process leaves." Envoy: "The hot restart functionality does not transfer existing connections to the new process." Confirms section 2's singleton, the listening socket given in one act and the successor accepting. Corrects section 2's "a connection to a database is a socket with a protocol over it ... and the handover moves both": no system read moves a connection, each drains it to its own end; a connection is a P5 by nature, and the matrix should say so for the client side as it does for the server side. Bears on (a).
- **A drain is long, graded, and ends by force, and the cost of a missed handover is measured** (*U13*). Envoy: `--drain-time-s` "Defaults to 600 seconds (10 minutes)", `--parent-shutdown-time-s` "Defaults to 900 seconds (15 minutes)"; with the gradual strategy "the probability that a response drains its connection increases linearly from zero at the start of the drain sequence to one at half of the --drain-time-s", and "Idle connections are closed once the drain timeout elapses". HAProxy before socket passing: "the rate of RSTs emitted during a reload was around 2-3 per million connections per reload", and with it "Failed requests: 0" of 2000 against 11 for a restart. Adds to section 7's "how long a drain is": the operator sets it in minutes, the system presses harder as it runs, and the end is a close, which the forwarder's end should be too. Bears on (a).
- **The way back is the old process kept whole until told, and compatibility is checked before the restart** (*U14*). nginx: "the old master process does not close its listen sockets, and it can be managed to start its worker processes again if needed"; rollback is "Send the HUP signal to the old master process. The old master process will start new worker processes without re-reading the configuration. After that, all new processes can be shut down gracefully, by sending the QUIT signal to the new master process." Envoy: `--hot-restart-version` "can be matched against the output of the GET /hot_restart_version admin endpoint to determine whether the new binary and the running binary are hot restart compatible". Confirms section 2's "the way back is the drain" and section 4's refusal before the deploy, in the operating-system precedent. Bears on (a), (c).

What the reader would tell the designers first: for a service whose state and mailbox the runtime holds, OTP already upgrades in place with no handover, drain or forwarder, paying only a suspend window and an untyped, hand-ordered appup, so take that shape for every process that owns no resource and derive the order from one atomic load and the plan from the hashes; keep the handover for the resource owner and the node that stops; and build the test with a previous build, a state written before and read after, and the downgrade, since the cost of testing by hand is the stated reason Ericsson's divisions, Elixir and WhatsApp restart instead.

What the reader could not verify: the WhatsApp sentences are HighScalability's summary of Rick Reed's 2014 talk, not the talk, and the 2012 WhatsApp slides say nothing about hot loading; no survey with numbers on how many Erlang or Elixir shops use hot upgrades was found; Elixir issue 8612 gives no reason for stopping, the reason quoted is the Mix documentation's; the HAProxy figures are Tarreau's own measurement on his setup; Envoy's documentation says nothing about shared-memory layout across versions beyond the version check; whether `mix castle.appup.gen` derives `{advanced, Extra}` entries or only `load_module` ones is not stated.

### Versioned actors in a cluster of mixed versions

The reader read Orleans's pages on grain interface versioning, compatible grains, the version selector strategy, deploying new versions, the backward-compatibility guidelines, serialization and its versioning rules, grain persistence and the grain lifecycle with grain migration, and issues 8439 and 8658 on `dotnet/orleans`; Akka's pages on typed actors, the style guide, typed persistence, schema evolution for event sourced actors, Jackson serialization and its schema evolution, serialization, rolling updates, the cluster `reference.conf` and the `SnapshotAdapter` API; Pekko's rolling updates page; Durable Functions' versioning pages; Dapr's `Dapr.Actors.Next` state migration page; and Service Fabric's note on actor type serialization.

- **Orleans versions the interface and leaves the state out, and says so in its first sentence** (*V1*). "In this article, you learn how to use grain interface versioning. The versioning of grain state is out of scope." (Grain interface versioning.) Adds: the closest typed mixed-version cluster has no counterpart to the matrix's C2; what a state change costs is left to the storage serializer's rules (*V6*), so the matrix's columns P3 and P4 are Ernest's own ground. Bears on (b), section 3.
- **Orleans's upgrade of a running activation is its recovery path and nothing more: the old activation is deactivated, the new one reads storage, and nothing in memory is handed over** (*V2*). "If the current activation isn't compatible, Orleans deactivates it and creates a new compatible one" (Grain interface versioning); "grains aren't always deactivated during some error cases (such as silo crashes). Therefore, applications shouldn't rely on the grain lifecycle always executing during grain deactivations" (Grain lifecycle); and a maintainer on issue 8658: "we strongly recommend to not write to storage on `OnDeactivateAsync`". Confirms section 2, *An upgrade is a recovery the operator chooses the moment of*: Orleans built exactly that, with the state outside. Corrects the same section's premise that the handover is cheap to add: Orleans has none, and the same issue shows what its absence costs, "once a silo is shutting down, all of his activations are considered dead ... Orleans might create a duplicate activation if a new request comes. It's a trade-off, between availability and consistency." Bears on (a), section 2, and claim 4 (one owner at every instant), which Orleans does not hold at a shutdown.
- **Orleans added a handover later, as grain migration: the state is dehydrated on one silo and rehydrated on another before activation, and some kinds cannot move** (*V3*). "Grain migration allows grain activations to move from one silo to another while preserving their in-memory state"; "Dehydration: Serializing the grain's state on the source silo before deactivation"; and "The following grain types cannot be migrated: Client grains, System targets, Grain services, Stateless worker grains" (Grain lifecycle). On issue 8658 the maintainer's answer to a drained rolling deploy is "a custom placement strategy (that would be aware of the deployment) to stop placing new activations on a silo that will be shut down" and then migration. Confirms section 2's handover, state by value to a successor on another node, and the kinds that cannot be moved, which are the matrix's P1 and the runtime's own processes. Adds: Orleans moves the state only when the grain is idle, `MigrateOnIdle()`, which is a drain of the current request and not of a mailbox; a hot grain is moved by the version check instead (*V4*). Bears on (a), section 2 *The handover* and *Across nodes*.
- **Compatibility is declared by the author and checked at the call, never inferred, and it is one-way: the new serves the old, and a new call to an old activation kills it** (*V4*). "Orleans doesn't infer at runtime which policy to use"; backward compatible means "All public methods present in the `Vm` version are also present in the `Vn` version" (Compatible grains); "a v1 grain can make calls to a v2 grain, but a v2 grain cannot call a v1 grain" (Grain interface versioning); and with `MinimumVersion` for staging, "V1 activations naturally 'migrate' to V2 silos eventually" (Deploy new versions of grains). Confirms section 4's *The plan from the hashes*: what Orleans has a human assert in `[Version(X)]` and a method-set rule, `ern diff` reads from the hashes, which is the gain. Corrects section 4's *The order of a rollout is free*: free for an old client meeting the successor, as the section says, but the reverse meeting, a new client and a service still on old code, is not served by any translation, and Orleans's answer to it is to deactivate the old activation on the first new-version call, so the key moves on demand; the plan should say what that meeting does in Ernest, refuse, wait or replace. Bears on (b), section 4.
- **Orleans's rolling upgrade breaks where a payload of a new type is decoded before the version is checked; open since 2023, with an outage reported** (*V5*). Issue 8439: "body deserialization is done before compatibility and version checking when received by the silo", with `Named type "MyObjectV2" is invalid`, and in 2024 on Orleans 8 the same as `Unable to resolve type alias`; "We just ran into this bug recently and it caused an outage in production." Adds: claim 2, no message of one version read as another's, must hold at the wire before any decoding, for the value types a new constructor carries and not only for the protocol's own type; a node that lacks a hash refuses the frame by the hash (`mvp3.1.md`'s exchange) and never reaches a decoder. Bears on (b) and section 5, claim 2.
- **The serializer's version-tolerance rules are the list of what a diff of two record types can derive, and field identity by number, not name, is what makes a rename free** (*V6*). "You can add or remove fields at any point in an inheritance hierarchy"; "You cannot change field IDs"; "You can change the *width* of a numeric field"; "You cannot change the *signedness* of a numeric field"; "Types with aliases can be renamed without breaking compatibility" (Serialization in Orleans). Adds to section 3's C2 and C3: an added field with a default, a removed field and a widened number are derivable; a rename is derivable only where the field keeps an identity the name is not, which the hash reader's *H5* already raised for types; everything else, a type change, a restructuring, is authored. Bears on (b).
- **Durable Functions pins a version to each running instance at creation, keeps every old code path alive in the one binary, and forbids changing it** (*V7*). "Each orchestration instance gets a version permanently associated with it when created"; "it's **critical** to preserve the exact orchestrator logic for older versions"; "Workers running newer orchestrator function versions can continue executing orchestration instances created by older versions. The runtime prevents workers running older orchestrator function versions from executing orchestrations of newer versions"; and with `Reject`, a mismatched instance "is rejected and returned to the work queue" (Orchestration versioning). Confirms claim 1, a process keeps its code unless it acts, and claim 6, every version in flight listed, since its retirement rule is "You checked through monitoring or querying that no instances are running with the legacy version." Adds: this is the alternative to `migrate` for a P5, no state crosses, both logics run, and the old is deleted when none is left; it is what §6.10 in place looks like when the identity is the whole value. Bears on (a), section 2 *In place is the exception*.
- **Akka Typed's handler returns the next behaviour, and the state rides in the behaviour's closure, so no one can ask an actor for its state** (*V8*). "Processing the next message then results in a new behavior that can potentially be different from this one. State is updated by returning a new behavior that holds the new immutable state" (Actors); the style guide puts the functional style where "The state is immutable and can be passed to 'next' behavior" and "The actor lifecycle has several different phases that can be represented by switching between different behaviors, like a finite state machine." Confirms (a)'s shape as a way to write a service. Corrects (a)'s hope that the shape removes the handover: in Akka the runtime holds the mailbox and the program holds the state in a closure, so a cluster rolling update moves no state; a sharded entity is stopped and "when an entity is moved the state will be restored" from persistence (Cluster Sharding). The handover is only removable where the runtime, not the closure, holds the state as a value of a declared type, which is what Ernest's `State` and the ask-for-it message give. Bears on (a), section 2 *What a replaceable service is*.
- **Akka Persistence refuses `become` for the actor whose state must survive, because a behaviour is state that recovery would have to rebuild** (*V9*). "The reason a new behavior can't be returned is that behavior is part of the actor's state and must also carefully be reconstructed during recovery. If it would have been supported it would mean that the behavior must be restored when replaying events and also encoded in the state anyway when snapshots are used. That would be very prone to mistakes and thus not allowed in Akka Persistence" (Persistence, *Changing Behavior*). Adds: the one actor shape in Akka built for a state that outlives a run is exactly (a)'s step function, `(state, command) -> effect` and `(state, event) -> state`, with the phase encoded in the state and the handler matching on it; a handover of such a state is a value, and a `become` beside it is a second state the recovery path does not see. So for P3 and P4 the step function is the right shape, and for P5 `become` with a translation is the exception it already is. Bears on (a), section 2 and the matrix's P5 column.
- **Akka's rolling update guarantees messages and stored data readable both ways, and nothing about actors; what it cannot read it logs and drops** (*V10*). "Compatibility of remote message protocols. Old nodes may send messages to new nodes and vice versa"; "Serialization format of persisted events and snapshots. New nodes must be able to read old data"; "If a `java.io.NotSerializableException` is thrown in `fromBinary` this is treated as a transient problem, the issue logged and the message is dropped" (Rolling Updates); `app-version` is "Used by rolling update features to distinguish between old and new nodes" and the default allocation "avoids allocating shards to old nodes during a rolling update" (`reference.conf`, Rolling Updates). Pekko is the same page under `pekko.cluster.app-version`. Confirms section 1: a rolling deploy is a restart with placement, and its whole content is what the wire and the store carry. Adds to section 3's C4: Akka's answer to a request the new code does not take is a dropped message, which the drain's translation improves on by answering in the old answer type's failure. Bears on (a), sections 1 and 3.
- **Akka's schema evolution derives the removal and the optional addition and writes the rest by hand, and a rollout with no degradation needs a build in between that reads the new form and still writes the old** (*V11*). "Removing a field can be done without any migration code"; "Adding an optional field can be done without any migration code"; a mandatory field, a rename and a restructuring are a `JacksonMigration` with `currentVersion` and `transform`; and "To complete a no-degradation rolling update, you need to make two deployments. First, deploy a new binary which can read the new schema but still uses the old schema. Then, deploy a second binary which serializes data using the new schema and drops the downcasting code from the migration" (Serialization with Jackson, *Rolling updates*). Confirms (b)'s split, the derivable and the authored. Corrects section 2's *The way back*, which asks the previous build to hold a `migrate` from the new state: the previous build was built before the new state existed, so the downcast can only be in the newer build, as Akka's `supportedForwardVersion` has it, or in a build made for the rollback. Bears on (b), section 2 *The way back*.
- **Event and snapshot adapters are `migrate` on stored state, run at the read and never at the deploy** (*V12*). "existing events stored in *Version 1* should be 'upcasted' to a new *Version 2* representation, and the process of doing so involves actual code" (Persistence, *Event Adapters*); `SnapshotAdapter.fromJournal` will "Transform the stored state into the current state type. Can be used for migrations from different serialized state types" (API); and the aim is to "Allow the system to continue operating without large scale migrations to be applied" and to "Transparently promote events to the latest versions during recovery" (Schema Evolution). Confirms section 2's *The migration is on that path already*. Adds the answer to section 7's question whether `migrate` for a stored state runs at the deploy or when read: every system read runs it at the read, and keeps the old form in the store until the next write. Bears on (b), section 7.

The reader read Avro 1.12's specification (Schema Resolution and Aliases); Confluent's Schema Registry page on schema evolution and compatibility; the proto3 language guide's "Updating A Message Type"; Buf's breaking-change rules and overview; the Elm guide's architecture and effects pages, the 2013 post "Interactive Programming" and the 0.18 post "The Perfect Bug Report", both from elm-lang.org's source, and elm/browser's `Debugger/Metadata.elm` and `Debugger/Report.elm`; Redux's "Configuring your store" and Elmish.HMR's page; safecopy's source and its Hackage page; Eliot Miranda's cogblog post on lazy become, the vm-dev threads of 2012 and 2014 on become's limit and cost, the squeak-dev thread on `ClassBuilder>>update:to:`, and the Kernel-eem.1323 commit of `updateInstances:from:isMeta:`; and Unison's post on its update process.

- **The derivable part of a migration is a short, closed list, and everything outside it is an error** (*S1*). Avro's Schema Resolution: "if the reader's record schema has a field that contains a default value, and writer's schema does not have a field with the same name, then the reader should use the default value from its field"; "if the reader's record schema has a field with no default value, and writer's schema does not have a field with the same name, an error is signalled"; "if the writer's record contains a field with a name not present in the reader's record, the writer's value for that field is ignored"; "the ordering of fields may be different: fields are matched by name"; "int is promotable to long, float, or double; ... string is promotable to bytes; bytes is promotable to string"; a missing enum symbol takes the reader's default "otherwise an error is signalled" (Avro specification 1.12). Bears on (b). Adds, to section 3's C2 and *What the program writes*: a `migrate` for C2 can be derived exactly where every field the new `State` adds has a default, every field it drops is dropped, and every type change is a promotion; the diff should sort a C2 into "derived" and "written", and the written part is the rest, never the whole.
- **A rename is undeterminable from the diff and is answered by an annotation on the new type** (*S2*). "Aliases function by re-writing the writer's schema using aliases from the reader's schema. If data was written as a record with a field named 'x' and is read as a record with a field named 'y' with alias 'x', then the implementation would act as though 'x' were named 'y'" (Avro specification, Aliases; reported from the 1.9.2 text, the 1.12 page paraphrases it). Bears on (b). Adds, to section 3's C2 and C3: a diff reads a rename as a removal and an addition, so a renamed constructor is a C4 and a renamed field a hand-written C2 unless the new declaration can say what it was; one word of annotation moves the commonest hand-written case into the derived set.
- **Compatibility has a direction, the direction fixes the rollout order, and the check runs at registration before anything is deployed** (*S3*). "BACKWARD: consumers using new schema can read data written with old schema"; "FORWARD: consumers using old schema can read data written with new schema"; FULL is "add/remove optional fields only"; "When schemas are updated, Schema Registry checks compatibility before accepting the new version"; under BACKWARD "upgrade all consumers before you start producing new events", under FULL "you can upgrade the producers and consumers independently" (Confluent, Schema Evolution and Compatibility). Bears on (a) and (c). Corrects section 4, *The order of a rollout is free*: it is free only because the successor serves both protocols, which is FULL; the plan should name the direction each translation gives, since a one-way translation, old to new, is BACKWARD, and then an old client's answers from the new service are the part the direction does not cover. Confirms section 4, *What is refused, and when*: the refusal at planning is the registry's refusal at registration.

"The Confluent Schema Registry default compatibility type is BACKWARD, not BACKWARD_TRANSITIVE"; "FULL_TRANSITIVE: backward and forward compatible between schemas X, X-1, and X-2" (Confluent). Bears on (c). Adds, to section 5, claims 6 and 7: a rolling deploy and a rollback put X-2 in flight beside X; the generated test should run against every version claim 6 lists as in flight, or the plan should say it checks X-1 only.
- **What makes protobuf's diff decidable is an identity for each part that survives editing, and a record of what was retired** (*S5*). "Changing field numbers for any existing field is not safe"; "Adding new fields is safe"; "Removing fields is safe"; "old binaries simply ignore the new field when parsing"; on removal "make the field number reserved, so that future users of your .proto can't accidentally reuse the number" (proto3 language guide). - **The Elm Architecture is the step function, and its runtime holds the state because effects are returned as values** (*S7*). - **Elm's hot swap is two steps and no forwarder, and a changed state type is refused, never migrated** (*S8*). "1. Compile the new program, resulting in a new signal graph. 2. ### Dynamic software updating as a field, and lazy migration

The reader read the Kitsune paper (OOPSLA 2012) in full; the TOPLAS version of *Mutatis Mutandis* (2007) in full, the POPL 2005 version being unreachable; the Ginseng technical report (CS-TR-4790); the Rubah paper (OOPSLA 2014); the Ekiden paper (HotSWUp 2011); Hayden's testing technical report (CS-TR-4949) and the page of its TSE 2012 version; Bierman, Hicks, Sewell and Stoyle's *Formalizing Dynamic Software Updating* (2003); Zenon's HotCI thesis (UCLouvain, 2024); the abstracts of Duggan's ICFP 2001 paper and of Anderson and Rathke's HotSWUp 2009 paper; and the Common Lisp HyperSpec, 4.3.6 with its three subsections, `update-instance-for-redefined-class` and `make-instances-obsolete`.

- **Every C and Java system puts the update point at the head of the long-running loop, where the program is "in between events", and Kitsune then restarts the whole program from `main` in the new version** (*D1*). "These are good choices for update points because the program is quiescent, i.e., in between events, when update-relevant state is not in the middle of being modified"; "The kitsune driver will load the new version and call its main function. During update execution, the program will direct control toward the equivalent update point in the new version" (Kitsune, §2.2). Bears on (a). Confirms section 2, *An upgrade is a recovery the operator chooses the moment of*: Kitsune is literally that, a fresh start of the new version with the old state migrated in. Adds: a step function from state and message to state has its update point at every message boundary by construction, which these systems had to find by hand in each loop, and had to retrofit blocking I/O to reach ("Rubah requires the program to either: (1) Use non-blocking sockets ... or (2) have each thread voluntarily wakeup from I/O calls frequently", Rubah §2.3).
- **Con-freeness is the condition, and it is about old frames on the stack, not about types in flight** (*D2*). "after a dynamic update to some type t, no updated values v′ of type t will ever be manipulated concretely by code that relies on the old representation. We call this property 'con-t-freeness'" (Mutatis, §1); the theorem is that a con-free, well-formed update preserves type soundness (§4.5, Theorem 4.2 and Lemma 4.3). The dynamic check "must examine the entire run-time execution context of the program, the code, stack, and heap", and its result "is unpredictable", so updates are confined to labelled points (§1); and "there is no assurance that such a location exists" (§5). Bears on (a). Confirms section 6's reading, that two types for two versions makes the condition trivial, with the reason stated: at a message boundary of a step function no frame of the old loop is active, so the only "code that relies on the old representation" is `migrate` itself, typed against the old type. Adds for `docs/soundness.md`: the obligation §6.10's tail call discharges is exactly "no old frame below the switch", and a step function discharges it by shape.
- **The field chose "representation consistency" and rejected coexisting versions of a type, for reasons that do not reach Ernest** (*D3*). Ginseng "enforces what we call representation consistency, in which all values of type T in the program at a given time must logically be members of T's most recent version. The alternative would be to allow multiple versions of a type to coexist" (Ginseng §2.2); Mutatis rejects copying because it "permits old and new code to manipulate different copies of the same logical data, which is likely to be disastrous in a language with side-effects" (§1). Bears on (a) and claim 2 of section 5. Adds: Ernest's "two versions of a type are two types" is the rejected alternative, passive partitioning, and it is sound here only because values are immutable and messages are copied; the price the field paid for consistency, every value transformed, Ernest pays instead as a translation at every boundary between versions, which is what the drain and the second key are. Say so in section 1, *Where Ernest starts*.
- **What a migration tool derives is the same rule in three systems: match by name and type, copy what matches, initialise what was added, drop what was removed, and leave the rest** (*D4*). Ginseng: "if a struct type had been extended by an extra field, the generator would produce code to copy the common fields and add a default initializer for the added one. This simplistic approach to patch generation is surprisingly effective, requiring few manual adjustments" (§5); Rubah's analyzer "matches fields by owner class name, field name, and field type. It generates a conversion method for each class with unmatched/changed fields that initializes those fields to a default value (0, false, or null). The developer then 'fills in the blanks'" (§2.4); CLOS: added local slots "are initialized ... according to their :initform forms", discarded slots are dropped and their values handed to the hook as a property list (CLHS 4.3.6.1, 4.3.6.2). Bears on (b). Confirms section 3's rows C2 and C3 and the tool of section 4. Corrects one thing: Rubah's and Ginseng's generated default is a silent value, which is the wart Ernest refuses; the derived `migrate` should leave a hole the checker refuses as it refuses a partial `match`, so that a deploy is refused at planning, as section 4 says, rather than running with a zero.
- **What a diff cannot see: a rename, and a changed meaning behind an unchanged type** (*D5*). Rubah's worked example is "Version v1 renames field writeCount to writeCountBase and introduces two new fields", so the generated stub zeroes the renamed field and the programmer copies it by hand (§2.4); and "Even if the set of fields is the same, with regards to name and type, the developer can define instance conversion methods to account for fields whose semantics changes" (§2.4). Bears on (b). Corrects section 3's matrix: a `State` whose hash is unchanged is row C1, "`migrate` the identity", but a loop that now reads a field differently needs a `migrate` all the same, and the hashes cannot show it; and a renamed field is C2 with a derivation that is wrong, not missing. The plan should let the program override a derived `migrate` for an unchanged `State`, and treat a removed-and-added pair of the same type as a question, never as drop-and-default.
- **The hand-written part stays small and is a one-time retrofit, in every measured system** (*D6*). Kitsune: "between 57 and 159 LoC total, across all versions of a program", xfgen specifications "totaling between 27 and 200 lines", "3–4 lines per changed variable or type" (§1, §4.1); Rubah: retrofit "from 29 to 267 lines of code and is a one-time effort", and "a total of 114 lines of code for all the updates we tested", at most 45 lines modified in any generated update class (§1, §5.1); Ginseng: "writing state transformers or adjusting type transformers was rarely needed" (§2). Bears on (b). Confirms section 3's *What the program writes, in all*: the bill is one function per changed type, and the retrofit, the update points and control migration, is what Ernest's step function removes entirely. Adds a measure for `experiments/code_update/`'s "written twice": the field's per-update cost is a few lines per changed type, so the comparison should count the retrofit, which is where Kitsune's and Rubah's lines went.
- **Locals are not roots: state that matters at an update lives in the loop's arguments, and the stack is thrown away** (*D7*). "We do not consider local variables to be roots, as their stacks are unwound during quiescence; our experience (with Rubah and Kitsune) is that values in locals at update-time are rarely needed" (Rubah §3.1). Ekiden found the hard part of a transferred state to be the function it held: "the serializers for a generic type needed to be aware of each of the type's uses" because the hash tables held function pointers (§III.B). Bears on (a) and (b). Confirms section 2's *What a replaceable service is*, a `State` the loop carries and nothing on the stack, and *Across nodes*, that a `State` holds no function: the two things the field found by experience are what the step function and §3.11 state by type.
- **Lazy migration buys a bounded pause and costs a discipline on the transformer: idempotent, pure, timing-independent** (*D8*). Rubah: with proxies "the pause time was typically 2–3 seconds, regardless of the heap size" against "tens of seconds to minutes" eagerly, but "all the conversion methods must be idempotent" since a lazy conversion may run twice (§1, §3.3); Ginseng: "the programmer has little control over when type transformers are invoked, since this is determined automatically. Therefore, transformers must be written to be timing-independent" (§2.2); Mutatis: "coercions should be written to act locally and avoid side-effecting computation" (§4.3); Kitsune's survey: lazy "could have the undesirable effect of delaying the pause until the application is performing a time-critical request" (§6). Bears on section 7's question whether `migrate` for a stored state runs at the deploy or when the state is next read. Adds: every cost of laziness is a cost on an impure or non-idempotent transformer, and an Ernest `migrate` is pure by type, so lazy is free of its usual rule for stored state; for a process's loop state there is one value and one call, so eager is the plain form and the lazy question is the keeper's and the table's alone.
- **CLOS migrates on touch, keeps identity, and hands the programmer an untyped property list and no initargs** (*D9*). "Updating such an instance occurs at an implementation-dependent time, but no later than the next time a slot of that instance is read or written"; "Updating an instance does not change its identity as defined by the function eq" (CLHS 4.3.6); the hook receives "a property list containing the slot names and values of slots that were discarded and had values" (4.3.6.2), and "no initialization arguments are passed to update-instance-for-redefined-class when it is called by the system" (4.3.6.3); `make-instances-obsolete` is invoked "automatically ... when defclass is used to redefine an existing standard class and the set of local slots accessible in an instance is changed or the order of slots in storage is changed" (its page). Bears on (b) and on section 7's lazy question. Adds the pitfalls the spec's own words give: an added slot's initform runs when the instance is next touched, not at the redefinition, so it sees that moment's environment; a discarded slot that was unbound leaves no trace in the list; the time is the implementation's; the hook is keyed to the class, so one redefinition obsoletes every instance of every subclass at once, which is the matrix's C5 by another route; and the hook is a plist, so nothing checks that the old value is read at its old type, which Ernest's `migrate : (State1) -> State2` does. In place and keeping identity, CLOS is the P5 column done for data rather than processes. *The Art of the Metaobject Protocol* was not reachable; nothing is claimed from it.
- **Erlang's "newest version on an external call" is a rule the formal model names, and Ernest drops** (*D10*). The 2003 calculus is "formulated as an extension of a typed lambda calculus, and supports updating technology similar to that of the programming language Erlang"; its semantics "is analogous to the prefixing semantics of Erlang, but is slightly more general. In particular, Erlang requires all references to an external module to invoke the newest version of the code; control is only afforded when referencing bindings within the same module" (§2.1). Bears on claim 1 of section 5. Confirms: a process that references code by hash never meets "the newest version" by accident, and takes new code only by acting, which the calculus had to add as a choice.
- **Typed hot swapping with a changed exported type was done by programmer-written version adapters, in 2001** (*D11*). Duggan's abstract: the problem is "to allow the new module to change the types exported by the original module, while preserving type safety", solved by "a reflective mechanism for dynamically adding type sharing constraints to the type system, realized by programmer-defined version adapters in the run-time" (ICFP 2001, abstract only). Bears on (b), rows C3 and C4. Confirms that the translation is the program's typed function and cannot be the tool's where a constructor's meaning changed; the C3 case, every constructor kept in form, is the part a tool writes. Read from the abstract alone; the mechanics are unverified.
- **A protocol of several messages has no safe point inside a session, and the field's answer for concurrent updates is a procedure that moves every party between sessions** (*D12*). Anderson and Rathke "present a procedure for updating multiple threads to cleanly migrate a system from one protocol to another", using session typing so that threads "communicate according to a given protocol" across "update boundaries which change the running protocol" (HotSWUp 2009, abstract only); Mutatis says a distributed update "would require coordination to prevent an updated program from sending a confusing message to a not-as-yet-updated one" (§1). Bears on section 4, *The order of a rollout is free*, and on section 7's drain-order question. Adds: section 4's claim holds for a protocol of independent requests, each translated on its own; where a service's protocol is a conversation, a request whose answer the client then follows with another, the translation of single messages does not make the order free, and the safe point is between conversations, which a step function does not see. The guide's rule of small, own protocols should say "of independent requests".
- **Without a timing restriction, 15% of updates applied at arbitrary points failed; at hand-chosen loop heads, none did** (*D13*). "1.48M of the total 9.8M tested executions failed (15%)" with no check; activeness safety cut this to 0.2% and con-freeness to 3.2%, while "selecting update points manually could avoid all failures while still permitting sufficient updates" (CS-TR-4949, §1, §2); the TSE version's page: "manual identification allowed none". Their method applies an update "at each distinct update point reached during execution of a suite of system tests", and where the output after a mid-run update may legitimately be either version's they build a "hybrid test" that accepts "the output of either P0 or P1" (§4). Bears on (a) and (c). Confirms (a): the hand-chosen loop head is the message boundary, so the step function gives every service the point that had no failures. Adds for (c): the systematic method is what `ern test` can run when the update point is a message boundary, apply the plan once at each boundary a test reaches; and the oracle is the problem, since a test that upgrades mid-run needs an expected result that may be either version's.
- **The identity upgrade is a test with no oracle, and a generated harness drives it; the state check is what no tool writes** (*D14*). Ekiden: "we found it useful to construct and test 'updates' from one version to the same version. This testing allowed us to check that the state necessary to resume execution was transferred" (§III.B), and Rubah measures a "v0v0 update" as its worst case (§5.2); HotCI's loop is "launching an Erlang/OTP release, using a separate Erlang process to interact with and modify its state, executing the upgrade, verifying that the state is correct, downgrading the release and asserting that the final state is as expected" (§1), and its author names the limit: "the inability to completely automate the writing of such tests. For instance, transforming the state from a version to another might involve complex business logic" (§1). Ekiden also found that "the code written to serialize and transform it accounted for the bulk of the modification effort" (§III.B). Bears on (c). Adds: the test of section 7's last question splits in two. The harness is derivable from the plan, start the previous build, drive it, apply the plan, drive the successor, apply the way back; the C1 case needs no oracle since the state after must equal the state before, and that runs on every commit; a C2 case's oracle is a property a human writes, and the cheapest is the round trip, the previous build's `migrate` back equalling the state it started from, which is section 2's way back tested for free.

What the reader would tell the designers first: the step function puts the service at the one place the whole field converged on, the head of the event loop, where con-freeness holds because no old frame is live, so write that argument in soundness.md and stop looking for a safe point; derive `migrate` by the three systems' shared rule but leave a hole, never a default, and say in the plan that the diff is blind to a rename and to a changed meaning behind an unchanged type; and generate the harness and the identity test now, since the oracle for a changed state is the one piece no system has automated.
