## The Runtime's Processes Wait on No Peer, 2026-11-18

MVP 4.16's item 104: a process of the runtime's that serves the whole node no longer waits on one peer. The shape was discussed first and decided with the user, and one agent built it.

**The problem.** The host holds a process that sends to another node while more than 1 MB waits for that node. A process of the runtime's that serves the whole node and sends a peer a signal of its own is held the same way, and everything it serves stops: every kill behind the killer, every spawn, monitor and `Down` behind the reaper, every exchange behind the exchange's process. *What a Full Buffer Holds* showed the killer's.

**Whatever frees the process leaves its signal waiting without a bound.** The host bounds what waits for a peer by holding the sender, and this sender must not be held. The signal then waits in the host's buffer, in a worker's mailbox or in a process made for the send, and nothing bounds any of the three. The buffer is the cheapest: one flag in the process, no message and no process, and a loss already drops what waits there.

**Decided: the host's `async_dist`.** A process that sets it is never held in a send to another node. Principle 3 asks for a mechanism, since a kill that does not end its process is a rule of Ernest's own failing at a limit (*A Connection That Cannot Drain*, its paragraph *Three kinds of promise at a limit*). Principle 1 picks this one: its reader knows processes as Erlang has them, and the flag is what Erlang gives a process that must not wait. It is the host's own function, which `CLAUDE.md`'s *Design* puts before anything cleverer.

The host counts nothing a flagged process places against the buffer's limit: its busy check reads `qsize`, which leaves those bytes out (`dist.c`, lines 1300 to 1331 and 3646 to 3653). So a program's `send` waits as before.

**Set aside.** A frame handed to the peer's worker costs a local message for each frame, and the same amount waits in the worker's mailbox. A process for each send needs room in a full table, which the killer exists to do without.

A worker that owned the monitors of its peer's processes, the asker waiting on the worker, would keep the host's hold on the asker. It would move the reaper's monitors across the seam where a loss, a `Down` and a `Restarted` are ordered (*No Restarted After a Death*).

**The narrower promise, taken for the monitors.** What the flagged processes send a peer waits without holding anyone, until the peer takes it or is lost. A program that loops over `monitor` and `demonitor` of a process of a peer that takes nothing adds two small signals a turn, and nothing stops it. Before, that loop held the reaper and with it the node. The reaper already keeps a row for every monitor a program makes, and a loop of monitors is bounded by nothing on one node either.

**What each sends.** A flagged process sends a peer one signal for one operation of a program's or one frame of the peer's, and never a stream of its own making. The killer sends a call's second note. Each frame of the exchange's answers a frame of the peer's, so a peer that takes nothing gets none. The reaper sends a monitor, a demonitor, a call's second note, and a compute's caller how the compute's process ended.

**The reaper's note to a compute's caller.** The first discipline was stronger: a flagged process sends nothing sized by a program's value. The build stopped at the reaper for it. A compute's process that foreign code ends with a term of its own has that term printed in the note to its caller, and nothing bounds the printing.

Decided with the user: the reaper sets the flag, and the discipline is the one above. The bytes are held either way. Without the flag the reaper is held at the first such note, and each later end waits in its mailbox with its reason. With it the same reasons wait printed in the peer's buffer, a few times larger. There is one note for each compute the peer asked for. Handing that note to the peer's worker would keep the first discipline, at the cost of machinery for a rare note.

**The clock and the departure.** *What a Full Buffer Holds* named both, and neither sets a flag. The clock sends a peer nothing: an alarm's process is its caller, on the clock's own node, and the clock's reference is private to its module. The departure waits for each peer's answer after its frame, at the latest until the loss, which §12.6 states. Held in the frame or in the answer, it waits on the same peer to the same moment.

Two runs of the killer's test against a stopped store passed on the old code, one with a message of 28 MB. That run is item 105's first evidence.

So the tests hold the desk's connection back: they suspend the host's process that writes the buffer to it, and nothing leaves the buffer, as nothing leaves it for a stopped peer once the kernel's buffers are full.
