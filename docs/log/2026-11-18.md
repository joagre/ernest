# The decisions log, 2026-11-18

## The Runtime's Processes Wait on No Peer, 2026-11-18

MVP 4.16's item 104: a process of the runtime's that serves the whole node no longer waits on one peer. The shape was discussed first and decided with the user, and one agent built it.

**The problem.** The host holds a process that sends to another node while more than 1 MB waits for that node. A process of the runtime's that serves the whole node and sends a peer a signal of its own is held the same way, and everything it serves stops: every kill behind the killer, every spawn, monitor and `Down` behind the reaper, every exchange behind the exchange's process. *What a Full Buffer Holds* showed the killer's.

**Whatever frees the process leaves its signal waiting without a bound.** The host bounds what waits for a peer by holding the sender, and this sender must not be held. The signal then waits in the host's buffer, in a worker's mailbox or in a process made for the send, and nothing bounds any of the three. The buffer is the cheapest: one flag in the process, no message and no process, and a loss already drops what waits there.

**Decided: the host's `async_dist`.** A process that sets it is never held in a send to another node. Principle 3 asks for a mechanism, since a kill that does not end its process is a rule of Ernest's own failing at a limit (*A Connection That Cannot Drain*, its paragraph *Three kinds of promise at a limit*). Principle 1 picks this one: its reader knows processes as Erlang has them, and the flag is what Erlang gives a process that must not wait. It is the host's own function, which `CLAUDE.md`'s *Design* puts before anything cleverer.

The host counts nothing a flagged process places against the buffer's limit: its busy check reads `qsize`, which leaves those bytes out (`dist.c`, lines 1300 to 1331 and 3646 to 3653). So a program's `send` waits as before.

**Set aside.** A frame handed to the peer's worker costs a local message for each frame, and the same amount waits in the worker's mailbox. A process for each send needs room in a full table, which the killer exists to do without.

A worker that owned the monitors of its peer's processes, the asker waiting on the worker, would keep the host's hold on the asker. It would move the reaper's monitors across the seam where a loss, a `Down` and a `Restarted` are ordered (*No Restarted After a Death*).

**The narrower promise, taken for the monitors.** What the flagged processes send a peer waits without holding anyone, until the peer takes it or is lost. A program that loops over `monitor` and `demonitor` of a process of a peer that takes nothing adds two small signals a turn, and nothing stops it. Before, that loop held the reaper and with it the node. The reaper already keeps a row for every monitor a program makes, and a loop of monitors is bounded by nothing on one node either.

**What each sends.** A flagged process sends a peer one signal for one operation of a program's or one frame of the peer's, and never a stream of its own making. The killer sends a call's second note. Each frame of the exchange's answers a frame of the peer's, so a peer that takes nothing gets none. The reaper sends a monitor, a demonitor, a call's second note, and a compute's caller how the compute's process ended.

**The reaper's note to a compute's caller.** The first discipline was stronger: a flagged process sends nothing sized by a program's value. The build stopped at the reaper for it. A compute's process that foreign code ends with a term of its own has that term printed in the note to its caller, and nothing bounds the printing.

Decided with the user: the reaper sets the flag, and the discipline is the one above. The bytes are held either way. Without the flag the reaper is held at the first such note, and each later end waits in its mailbox with its reason. With it the same reasons wait printed in the peer's buffer, a few times larger. There is one note for each compute the peer asked for. Handing that note to the peer's worker would keep the first discipline, at the cost of machinery for a rare note.

**The clock and the departure.** *What a Full Buffer Holds* named both, and neither sets a flag. The clock sends a peer nothing: an alarm's process is its caller, on the clock's own node, and the clock's reference is private to its module. The departure waits for each peer's answer after its frame, at the latest until the loss, which §12.6 states. Held in the frame or in the answer, it waits on the same peer to the same moment.

**A probe with OTP alone.** Two plain nodes over loopback, the peer stopped and the buffer full, two runs of two. A process with the flag made a monitor, a demonitor and a send in 4 to 34 µs each, where a plain process's monitor and a plain small send were held. An exit given to a process the host held in a send ended it within 51 µs, also when `suspend_process` held it too, as the killer has its victim.

**The holds, shown.** Each hold was shown on the code without the flag before the flag went in. A stopped peer could not show one for certain. The host places a message above 64 KB in fragments, and only while less than its limit waits. After the stop the kernel's buffers and the TLS sender take several megabytes in steps, so a process held at one step is let go at the next. Two runs of the killer's test against a stopped store passed on the old code, one with a message of 28 MB. That run is item 105's first evidence.

So the tests hold the desk's connection back: they suspend the host's process that writes the buffer to it, and nothing leaves the buffer, as nothing leaves it for a stopped peer once the kernel's buffers are full. On the old code the killer's test and the reaper's two waited 30 seconds for a `Down` that never came, and the exchange's spawn on a third node answered `Left(Io.Timeout)`. With the flag each passed three times of three, in 1.4 to 2.3 seconds.

**The killer's flag frees more than kills.** On the old code the reaper also waits behind a held killer, since the end of a delivery waits for its kill's `Down`.

**The list is held.** `docs/design/nodes.md` names the processes that set the flag, and `unwaiting_processes_test` holds its list equal to every function of the sources that names `async_dist`.

**A helper's comment.** `ern_waits:queued/2` said the host traces a message put in a suspended process's mailbox. On OTP 29 it traces it only as the process next runs, two runs of two. The helper's one use sends before it asks, so its first read holds. The comment says what is so, and the exchange's test watches the gateway's send for the same reason.

**The read-back's two searches.** Three test programs close a held connection the same way, in two lines. They stay: what stands between differs at each, and a function that took it would nest where the reader reads a sequence. No private function of the tests' Ernest names only the library's types.

**Not shown.** A peer that is really stopped, under the new code. The reaper's second note of a call and its note to a compute's caller under a hold. The exchange's lacks and code frames under one. A peer's kill and a compute's kill. Whether a peer's worker sends a node other than its own peer anything the host may hold: the sends read go to its own peer or stay on the node, and its code was not read whole.

**The workers' reading, planned.** Decided with the user after the close. A worker serves one peer so that one peer's wait holds no other's frames, and a worker that sent a third node a signal the host may hold would break that for its own peer. It is MVP 4.16's item 108, a reading by an agent, standing after item 106.

**What it cost.** The build 55 minutes of one agent. `make test-full` ran once at the close.

## Suspended Is the Host's Word, 2026-11-18

MVP 4.16's item 107: `Process.status` says `Suspended` of a process the host holds. The decision and its argument are *What a Full Buffer Holds*, its paragraph *`Process.status`*. One agent built it.

**The report.** E.21 gains the constructor, last in `Activity` and with no field, and a paragraph naming the three holds: a `send` to another node, or a call or a wait on a peer that waits as a `send` waits; a `kill` the runtime carries out; and a write to one of the node's own ports that takes no more. A process the host holds is `Suspended` whatever it waited for. §11.2 names the listing's fourth word, `suspended`, which the order of the words puts last in a site. The guide's deployment chapter names it too, and its paragraph on `:processes` became three at its turns.

**The kill's clause, narrowed after the build.** The first sentence said a killed process is `Suspended` until it has ended. The killer lets the process go once the exit is on its way, and the host may say `exiting` of it then, which reads `Running`. The sentence now says while the runtime carries out the kill, which is true of every read.

**Built.** `ern_rt:activity/4` reads the host's status `suspended` before any mark, so a held caller reads `Suspended` and not `Calling`. The runtime suspends a process in one place, the killer's, so the three holds are all there are. `Process.Activity` gains the constructor, and the listing's `word` its word. No reader of a status in the repository matched every constructor, so nothing else changed.

**The tests.** Each failed on the old clause, which read `Running`. A unit test holds three processes by the host's own suspension, one in a receive, one in a call and one in a wait on a peer: each reads `Suspended` while held, and what it read before once let go. A second holds a running program's process at its helper's port. The helper is stopped by its signal, and of two writes of 1 MB the second is held: the process reads `Receiving`, `Suspended`, and `Receiving` once the helper continues, ten runs of ten beside 24 busy loops. *What a Full Buffer Holds* had seen this hold only by hand.

Across nodes the desk's connection to the store is held back, as the killer's test holds it, and the desk reads its held sender as `Some(Process.Suspended)`. The test takes 1.6 s and waits for no detector. The listing's two cases gain a suspended process, one for its line and one for its place after `running`.

**No test for the kill, nor for a call held.** Weighed with the user after the close, and left, since each would hold nothing. A kill under way cannot be read without a race. The test that can be written traces the killer's calls and holds their order, which says again what `killing/2`'s three lines say.

A call, a find, a spawn, a compute and a utilization are held by the host as the sender the nodes' test reads is, and their marks are the unit test's. Each link is held, so a test of the chain would fail only where one of them does. A peer that is really stopped is `wait_ended_by_a_loss`'s, which shows that the host suspends its sender.

**Left as it is.** The shell tests' `listed/3` lets a line it expects `receiving` or `calling` be `running`, and not `suspended`. Only a process whose kill is under way could be listed so, and no test lists a process it kills and expects it to wait.

**The guide's frame.** The guide's §6.3 and §6.4 quote the library's frame in a trace, `Process.run:296`, by its line in `process.ern`. The new paragraph of `Activity`'s comment moved it from 289, and the guide's test, which the full run alone holds, named it.

**The read-back's two searches.** The form `Optional.map(Process.status(p), fn(status) = status.activity)` stands at two sites, the library's example and the nodes' test, which is no repeat to name. The one private function the item wrote, the test program's `reads`, names the program's own message types.

**The type lines, planned.** No test holds Appendix E's `type` lines to the library: `listings_are_the_interfaces_test` reads the functions' lines alone. E.21's line for `Activity` is equal to `process.ern`'s by a reading. Decided with the user after the close: it is MVP 4.16's item 109, standing after item 108.

**What it cost.** The build 21 minutes of one agent. `make test-full` ran once at the close.

## A Home of the Run's Own, 2026-11-18

MVP 4.16's item 87, what *The Shell's Tests in a Home of Their Own* left. Two agents built it, and the first showed what the plan's guard holds before it built.

**Shown.** A scratch home's startup file printed one line. In a scratch state of the tests, 209 tests started a shell under it, and 4 failed: `session`, whose output is compared whole; `early_keys` and `early_paste`, which read the line after the start line; and `refusal_color`, at an expect. The other 205 passed with the line in their output, since each asserts a text present, absent or counted, an expect, a file or standard error. So the plan's shape, a line alone, held 4 of 209.

**A second probe.** With `:quit` after the line, 206 of the 209 failed, a terminal test in about a second. Two of the three left end before any startup file runs, and one asserts only an absence.

**The narrow promise.** The run's home alone removes the defect at its cause: no shell test runs the person's startup file, whether the test fails or not. It does not hold that a test names a home of its own, and the terminal sessions of tests that name none write one history file.

**The wider one, decided with the user.** The startup file ends the session too, so a test that names no home fails at its first assertion and its session writes no history. It costs one line. A harness that refused output holding the line would say why it failed, but would put the shell's rule into `ern_pty`, which other areas' tests use. The cost that stays: the failure does not say why. EUnit prints `nomatch` or a cut value, and the line shows once the output is printed.

**Built.** `test/Makefile`'s `shell` target is the one way the two modules are run. It makes a home under the run's own directory, copies `test/fixtures/session/startup` into it, and runs the test host under it. `inherited_home_test_`, the one test besides `no_home` that names no home, reads the line and holds that the session ended before its input, so a run outside the home fails. Every `ern` a shell test starts runs under the run's home, a build and a node among them, and the host reads no `~/.erlang`.

**What the guard lets through.** A session that ends before its startup files run, which reads nothing of a home. And a session whose standard output is closed: its greeting's failed write and the startup's inputs race, and the inputs lost 21 rounds of 21 in the second agent's probe.

**Three starts outside the shell's tests.** `ern_integration_tests`' `closed_pipe` started two shells under the inherited home and `ern_install_tests`' `install` one. Shown by a probe home: the install's session ran the startup's inputs, and the two into a closed pipe read the file and set the home's `.ernest` to its owner's alone, so the tests changed the person's own directory. Each now names a home among its test's files. A fourth start, `closed_stream`'s, ends in `bin/ern` before the host starts, and stays.

**The full run under a probe home.** `make test-full` ran once with `HOME` a home that holds the fixture's startup file. It passed in 356 s, and the file's access time stood before the run's start, where a read moves it. So no start of the full run reads the inherited home's startup file. Nothing holds that outside `make test-shell`: this probe is the way to see it.

**A plain test.** `listed_by_memory_test` was the module's one test that started a session under EUnit's own limit of five seconds, with no count of its steps. It is counted as the others are.

**What it cost.** The first agent took 15 minutes and the second 7. `make test-shell` took 26 s before and 27 s after.
