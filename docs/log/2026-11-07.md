# The decisions log, 2026-11-07

## Nothing at the Wait, 2026-11-07

MVP 4.15's item 2, decided in a design session after *A Wait Collects* was reopened. The question was what gates the collection at a wait. The answer is that there is no collection at a wait: a process that waits holds what it dropped until it runs again, as the host's does (§10).

**Why the gate could not be fixed.** Both warts had one cause. The gate tried to know, without collecting, whether a collection would free anything, and a count cannot tell a dropped binary from a kept one. The one rule without that guess bounds the cost by the wait instead of by evidence, a wait in proportion to the heap, and a constant remains to choose in it. Measured on nike before the decision, OTP 29: a full sweep costs 0.5 to 3.2 ns a word of heap, 30 ms for 148 MiB of list cells; a burst that builds and drops a list costs 2.3x to 6.4x with a collection after it, since the collection shrinks the heap and the next burst regrows it, 0.45 to 3 ns a word of the heap dropped; a count of the mailbox and `total_heap_size` costs 47 ns against 749 for `garbage_collection_info`. So a rule that collected any idle process, with a millisecond's wait for each so many words of heap, was within reach.

**Why nothing instead.** Against the host, doing nothing is no worse at all. An idle Ernest process holds what an idle Erlang process holds, dropped binaries up to the host's `bin_vheap` limit, 371 KiB and up, and heap garbage up to its heap, until it runs again or ends. Erlang leaves that to the program, with `hibernate_after` and `garbage_collect`. Principle 1 decides: the reader knows processes as Erlang has them, and the mechanism was Ernest departing from the host on every wait to pay for one program's idle connections. The §0 rule on cost agrees, since the host's behavior is the floor and not something to beat: the timer charged every blocking wait, the counter program from 1.26x to 1.68x against Erlang and the echo's round trip from 541.7 to 771.3 ns, the largest cost one item had added to a round trip. And the rule had been wrong twice in a day.

**The three references.** A response passes through the socket's process and its writer, so three processes reference the bytes where the host's program holds one. The bytes are stored once, and go when the last reference is collected. Collecting the runtime's two at their operations' end was weighed and set aside: it frees nothing while the program's process holds its own reference, which under this rule it does until it runs again.

**What stays.** The host's process of a connection over TLS hibernates after a millisecond's wait, by the host's `hibernate_after`, which E.18 states now. It is the host's own option on the host's own process, which Ernest reaches no other way, set once by the runtime as a listener's `SO_REUSEADDR` is; its cost was measured within the spread, and the millisecond is the host's shortest timer. Without it an idle TLS connection holds 2 MiB of the host's that no program could free. The two defects fixed with *A Wait Collects* stay with their regression tests, the frame at a wait naming the receive's line and `Code.running` finding a tail timed receive. Whether a compiled receive stays a fun applied where it stands, without the collection's second wait, the item session reads, and keeps it only where a defect fix rests on it.

**A program's own collection.** A function by which a program asks for a collection or a hibernation, as an Erlang program can, is dropped and not kept for later. No program asks for it; a server's keep-alive timeout bounds what its idle connections hold, since a closed socket's process ends and holds nothing; and E.0 weighs it the day a program asks. ernest-httpd's caps are its own, and it hears at the milestone's end that no archive carries a collection at the wait.

**The cost, stated.** The numbers of *A Wait Collects* stand as the cost of this rule: about 2 MiB an idle connection after a 1 MiB response, 206 MiB for 100, until the connection closes or runs again. Items 3 and 4 show and say it, so that an operator reads what an idle process holds where the host's own operators read it.
