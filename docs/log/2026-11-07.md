# The decisions log, 2026-11-07

## Nothing at the Wait, 2026-11-07

MVP 4.15's item 2, decided in a design session after *A Wait Collects* was reopened. The question was what gates the collection at a wait. The answer is that there is no collection at a wait: a process that waits holds what it dropped until it runs again, as the host's does (§10).

**Why the gate could not be fixed.** Both warts had one cause. The gate tried to know, without collecting, whether a collection would free anything, and a count cannot tell a dropped binary from a kept one. The one rule without that guess bounds the cost by the wait instead of by evidence, a wait in proportion to the heap, and a constant remains to choose in it. Measured on nike before the decision, OTP 29: a full sweep costs 0.5 to 3.2 ns a word of heap, 30 ms for 148 MiB of list cells; a burst that builds and drops a list costs 2.3x to 6.4x with a collection after it, since the collection shrinks the heap and the next burst regrows it, 0.45 to 3 ns a word of the heap dropped; a count of the mailbox and `total_heap_size` costs 47 ns against 749 for `garbage_collection_info`. So a rule that collected any idle process, with a millisecond's wait for each so many words of heap, was within reach.

**Why nothing instead.** Against the host, doing nothing is no worse at all. An idle Ernest process holds what an idle Erlang process holds, dropped binaries up to the host's `bin_vheap` limit, 371 KiB and up, and heap garbage up to its heap, until it runs again or ends. Erlang leaves that to the program, with `hibernate_after` and `garbage_collect`. Principle 1 decides: the reader knows processes as Erlang has them, and the mechanism was Ernest departing from the host on every wait to pay for one program's idle connections. The §0 rule on cost agrees, since the host's behavior is the floor and not something to beat: the timer charged every blocking wait, the counter program from 1.26x to 1.68x against Erlang and the echo's round trip from 541.7 to 771.3 ns, the largest cost one item had added to a round trip. And the rule had been wrong twice in a day.

**The three references.** A response passes through the socket's process and its writer, so three processes reference the bytes where the host's program holds one. The bytes are stored once, and go when the last reference is collected. Collecting the runtime's two at their operations' end was weighed and set aside: it frees nothing while the program's process holds its own reference, which under this rule it does until it runs again.

**What stays.** The host's process of a connection over TLS hibernates after a millisecond's wait, by the host's `hibernate_after`, which E.18 states now. It is the host's own option on the host's own process, which Ernest reaches no other way, set once by the runtime as a listener's `SO_REUSEADDR` is; its cost was measured within the spread, and the millisecond is the host's shortest timer. Without it an idle TLS connection holds 2 MiB of the host's that no program could free. The two defects fixed with *A Wait Collects* stay with their regression tests, the frame at a wait naming the receive's line and `Code.running` finding a tail timed receive. Whether a compiled receive stays a fun applied where it stands, without the collection's second wait, the item session reads, and keeps it only where a defect fix rests on it.

**A program's own collection.** A function by which a program asks for a collection or a hibernation, as an Erlang program can, is dropped and not kept for later. No program asks for it; a server's keep-alive timeout bounds what its idle connections hold, since a closed socket's process ends and holds nothing; and E.0 weighs it the day a program asks. ernest-httpd's caps are its own, and it hears at the milestone's end that no archive carries a collection at the wait.

**The cost, stated.** The numbers of *A Wait Collects* stand as the cost of this rule: about 2 MiB an idle connection after a 1 MiB response, 206 MiB for 100, until the connection closes or runs again. Items 3 and 4 show and say it, so that an operator reads what an idle process holds where the host's own operators read it.

## Nothing at the Wait Built, 2026-11-07

MVP 4.15's item 2, built as *Nothing at the Wait* says. The collection went whole, and the fixtures, the examples, the guide and the tour stand as they did before *A Wait Collects*. What stays of that commit is E.21's run of identical lines, the two fixes below and the host's `hibernate_after`, now a constant of `ern_tcp` beside E.18's sentence.

**Whether a compiled receive stays a fun.** *Nothing at the Wait* left this to the build: the fun stays only where a defect fix rests on it. A receive with an `after` stays a fun, since its deadline enters it again. Both defects *A Wait Collects* named are that fun's: its frame taken for a helper's with its line lost, and `Code.running` missing it in a function's tail. A receive without an `after` is the host's `receive` again.

**What that gives back.** *A Wait Collects* also moved a plain receive's waiting frame to the receive's own line, which it called a correction beside its two defects. *Nothing at the Wait* names that line among what stays, and this narrows it. A timed frame keeps the receive's line. A plain frame returns to the last call before the wait or its function's head, `Dead.work:13` and not `Dead.work:14`.

**What the line rested on.** It rested on the collection's second wait alone. The host records a waiting process at the top of the receive's loop, so its frame names the last line before that loop. The host's compiler inlines a fun applied at once that nothing calls again: `fun Wait() -> receive ... end end()` compiled to the plain receive's instructions, with its line. The one form left that holds the line is a recursion that never runs, `fun Wait(After) -> receive ... after After -> Wait(After) end end(infinity)`, in every receive of every program.

**Why not that form.** Its cost does not decide: the host compiles it to a local call, 529 ns a round trip against 524, within the spread. Principle 1 decides. The reader knows processes as Erlang has them, this item's argument is that Ernest's wait is the host's, and E.21 has stated the plain frame's line since 2026-10-23 (*What the Skeptics Found*). Principle 3's "a receive at the line that receives" is about the program's text, which shows it, and the report's line still names the function the process waits in.

**What had stood contradicted.** *A Wait Collects* changed no sentence on this. It moved §12.4's example and the traces alone, so E.21, the guide's §5.4, `why.md` and the tour stood against their own blocks from that commit to this one.

**A fault at a wait.** Reading the forms found a defect. A fault taken at a wait, a foreign message's (§8.4) or one met on a message's way (§6.5), wrote its frame with no line, since the `throw` the compiler writes held none. It now holds the receive's line, with an `after` and without. A line costs a row of the host's table and no instruction. E.21 states this, with the two cases it was silent on: no call before the wait, and a receive with an `after`.

**The TLS test.** `written_bytes_are_freed_over_tls_test` read every process of the run, and the socket's process and its writer now hold their bytes, as §10 says. It becomes `tls_connection_hibernates_test`, which reads the host's processes alone. `ern_waits:with_room/1` and `freed/2` stay for it. So does `docs/memory_checks.md`'s line on reading a process's references, since the trap it names is the host's and no part of the rule.

**The tests, set aside.** Each regression test of the item ran with the code it guards set aside, and failed: the TLS option, the timed fun's line, the `throw`'s line, the frame's name, a run of lines written once, `Code.running`'s reading of a wait and the lambda's wait's name. `running_test`'s lambda gained an `after`, since `LambdaWait` now names a timed receive alone and the test no longer reached it.

**A timeout that was no defect.** The TLS test timed out in the item's first full run, the host's sender holding its megabyte unhibernated, which read as a defect of OTP's. It was a beam of this session's: the option had been set aside for the run above, and the file put back with its old time, so `make` kept the beam built without it. OTP's `ssl` alone hibernated in 12 runs of 12, quiet and under load, and so did Ernest's flow once rebuilt. E.18's sentence stands, and `docs/memory_checks.md` now names the trap.

**The numbers.** `make bench` on nike against the release's reference, then `make bench-programs` three times and `make bench-call` twice, since one run of either decides nothing.

## Memory Shown, 2026-11-07

MVP 4.15's item 3. `Process.status` has a `memory` field, the bytes a process holds, and `:processes` writes it after the activity, `Counter.serve receiving 3120 bytes`, so that an operator reads what an idle process holds (*Nothing at the Wait*).

**Against E.0.** Rule 1 admits it, since the count is the host's. It is a field of `status` and no function of its own, because `status` is already the snapshot of a process and already asks the host for it, so a second function would be a second read of the same process. Rule 3 refuses a buried policy, and what the count counts is a choice, so E.21 states it whole. Shape rule 10 names it `memory`, the host's word.

**Which count.** The host's `memory` leaves binaries out, 2,632 bytes for a process that holds a megabyte. Two reads give them: `process_info`'s `binary`, the list that the host's own operators' tools sum, and `garbage_collection_info`'s `bin_vheap_size` and `bin_old_vheap_size`. They agreed on a binary kept, dropped, used in part, waiting in the mailbox and referenced twice. They differ by the words: the collector counts a 100-byte binary as 96 bytes. The list grows with the references, so it stands against CLAUDE.md's rule on scans, and the count does not. Principle 5 decided, the host's own count with nothing kept beside it.

| Read of a waiting process, by another, nike | ns |
|---|---|
| as it was, no memory | 1617 |
| with `memory` | 1689 |
| with `memory` and `binary` | 1910 |
| with `memory` and the collector's counts | 2597 |
| holding 10,000 binaries, `memory` and `binary` | 508,083 |
| holding 10,000 binaries, `memory` and the counts | 2126 |

So a read of `status` costs about 60% more, a constant. Nothing calls it on a hot path: the supervisors call it on a retry, and the shell at a command.

**The writer.** A real connection showed what the plan did not name. A socket's writer has no row, and it holds what it wrote until it runs again. After a 1 MiB response and an upload of 32 MiB on one connection, the socket's process had collected, the program's process had too, and the writer alone held the megabyte, the node's binaries at 1,285 KiB with no line showing it. A socket is one process to E.18, so its memory counts its writer's. The socket's process keeps the writer under a key, as it keeps nothing else, and only a socket pays the second read. Before the upload the one binary counts twice in the socket's line, `Tcp.accept 2102984 bytes`, once for each process, as E.21 says. Counting it once needs the scan.

**What is not counted.** The host's processes of a connection over TLS are the host's, and hibernate (E.18). A binary that code holds is no process's: a shell's binding is a literal of its holder, so a process that only captures one shows 2,816 bytes, which is the truth and read as a defect until the holder was read. E.21 says so.

**A read moves nothing.** 100,000 reads of `Process.status` left a dropped megabyte where it was. `docs/memory_checks.md` and `ern_waits:freed/2` said that a read in a loop fills and collects its target. That did not reproduce: 27 targets with their heaps at every fill up to 260 words, 10,000 reads each, lost no binary. The sentence goes from both. `freed/2` stays, as a wait on a traced collection and no loop.

**The order.** `:processes` sorted its lines as text, so a count would have sorted as text too. One site's processes now go by activity and then by memory as a number, which §11.2 states.

**The tests.** `make test-shell`, the runtime's and the deployment guide's sessions hold the listing, the last two masking a count that differs from run to run. Each new regression test ran with its code set aside and failed: the binaries counted, the writer counted, and the order by memory.

**The guide.** Item 4 is one sentence at the end of the deployment guide's section 7: no line says what a process holds, one that waits keeps what it dropped, and `:processes` shows it.

## `kill` Takes a `Process`, 2026-11-07

MVP 4.15's item 5, feedback 213. `kill` takes a `Process`, as `monitor` and `demonitor` do, and `Process.kill` goes.

**Why.** *`kill` and `monitor` Keep the Address* held that killing is authority and an address the authority over a process. *`Process.kill`* of 2026-10-31 gave that up, by principle 1: a process the operator can see and not end is one the operator cannot act on. Since then any holder of a `Process` could end it, so the address granted nothing, and the two kills were one verb on one kind, which E.0's shape rule 2 no longer admits. Principle 2 allows the standard library to pair functions, so it did not decide this alone. Principle 1 did: a reader who knows `monitor(process, wrap)` predicts `kill(process)`, and the guide's style already says that a prelude name means the prelude's thing in every module, `kill` among them, which `Process.kill` broke.

**What does not change.** §6.9's rest stands: a `kill` is asynchronous, does nothing to a process that has ended, and reaches another node as a `send` does. `Process.fromAddress` goes through every adapted address, as `kill` did, so a kill ends the same process it ended before. §6.5 stands too: an address is the permission to send, and a kill is no send.

**The cost, stated.** A site that holds only an address writes `kill(Process.fromAddress(worker))`, the common case by far: 21 sites of the repository's Ernest, 8 of them in the standard library, the shell and the examples, and three lines of the tour's first sessions grow by it. Only two functions here monitor and kill one address, both test programs, so what the item buys is one way to kill and not a shorter function. A `kill` that took either would be the second spelling again. ernest-httpd already writes `Process.kill(Process.fromAddress(...))` at 14 sites, and becomes shorter.

**What it touched.** `Code.running` still answers addresses, since an address is what moves a process, so killing those it finds writes `fn(p) = kill(Process.fromAddress(p))`. `docs/man/` is written from main with this commit, since the site's test runs its blocks. The runtime's `kill` takes a pid and no longer resolves an address, so a test or a bench that holds an adapted address passes it through `process_of` first, as `Proxy.start`'s row does.

## Text from Bytes, 2026-11-07

MVP 4.15's item 6, feedback 206. `String.fromUtf8Lossy` and `String.fromUtf8Piece` are built, the two policies of `fromUtf8` that E.0's rule 3 admitted on 2026-11-02, and the shell's `foreign fn decoded` and the harness's own decoding go.

**The form.** Ernest closes the difference around the host's decoder. The decoder answers a binary, `{incomplete, Text, Cut}` or `{error, Text, Rest}`, a host term no Ernest type describes, so one adapter, `ern_string:decoded/1`, gives it a private type's two shapes (E.0 rule 1). `fromUtf8Piece` is Ernest over that, and `fromUtf8Lossy` is `fromUtf8Piece` with each byte left over shown as U+FFFD.

**What the Erlang hid.** `ern_shell:shown/1` copied everything after a stray byte at each stray byte, so it was quadratic. On nike 100,000 stray bytes took 4.8 s, the screen's process doing nothing else meanwhile. The Ernest collects its parts and joins them once: 57 ms for 100,000 and 887 ms for a million.

**What the host keeps.** The decoder calls a byte `incomplete` by its form alone: `<<245>>` and `<<192>>` wait though no character can begin so, where `<<255>>` is stray at once. E.5 states this and the code leaves it. To close it, Ernest would have to carry UTF-8's table of first bytes, to show a U+FFFD one write sooner, and the text a reader ends with is the same (principle 2). A test cuts a mixed text at every place and reads the whole.

**What §11.2 had left silent.** The bytes of a character a write ends inside wait for the next write to either stream, and go with the session where none comes. The code had always done so, a comment saying it. §11.2 now does.

**The cost, stated.** The plain form adds about 50 ns to the decoder on a piece, 69 ns against 31 at 10 bytes, 103 against 49 at 100 and 311 against 275 at 1,000. The reverse and the join over one part are most of it. A special case for one part would be code only to go faster, and a piece comes off a socket or a port, whose read costs far more, so it stays plain.

**Sites swept.** Five lines that showed a program's output through `Optional.withDefault(String.fromUtf8(bytes), "")`, which lost all of it to one bad byte, read `String.fromUtf8Lossy(bytes)`: the language guide's, `Os.closeInput`'s example, two of `tools/site/run.ern` and a test's. A test helper that marks bad output with `"?"` stays, since the marker is its point. The compiler's diagnostic keeps its Erlang, since the compiler calls no shim.

## A Text after Its Prefix, 2026-11-07

MVP 4.15's item 7, feedback 214, 215, 220 and 227, one reading of `String` and `Bytes` against E.0's rules. It admits `String.drop`, `Bytes.drop`, `String.stripPrefix` and `String.stripSuffix`.

**`String.drop` reverses 2026-09-26.** That day's reading refused it under rule 4, as `slice` with `size`. The verdict read the spelling and not the walk. `size` walks the whole text and `slice` walks to its end, so a scan that slices to the end grows with the square of the text, where `drop` walks only what it drops. The private primitive has stood for that reason since 2026-10-01, beneath `trimStart` and `split`, so exporting it asks the host for nothing new. Rule 1 decides: an operation that its Ernest would make grow where the host does not is a primitive. Rule 2 gives it `List.drop`'s word and count, and the text's vocabulary now lists it.

**`Bytes.drop` is principle 5's.** On `Bytes` no walk is saved, since `size` is constant. A reader who knows `String.drop` looks for it there, and principle 5 says a function is where that reader looks. `Bytes.slice(bytes, index, Bytes.size(bytes))` also names its subject twice.

**`stripPrefix` and `stripSuffix` are not compositions.** Each answers `None` where the text does not begin or end so, a case of its own, which rule 4 allows. Rule 3 admits them as general operations with an obvious definition, and shape rule 4 gives them `Optional`. They search as `startsWith` and `endsWith` do, by whole graphemes. The word is new because no verb of rule 2 does this: `trimStart` removes whitespace and `remove` goes by key. Two private `stripped` helpers go.

**What stays out.** `String.take` is `slice(text, 0, count)`, one call that names its subject once, so rule 4's verdict stands and principle 2 keeps out the second spelling. `dropLast` and `takeLast` on text stay `slice` with `size`, with no walk saved, since `size` must walk in any case. `Bytes.stripPrefix` stays out because a pattern already states a constant prefix, `<<'\r':utf8, rest:bytes>>`, and `startsWith` with `drop` a variable one.

**Feedback 215 is dropped.** `String.contains(x, "\r")` finds nothing in `"\r\n"`, by E.5's rule that a search matches whole graphemes. A search by code points beside it would be a second way to search (principle 2), and `toList` reads code points already, as `libs/http`'s renderers do. E.5 now says so in a sentence, and `String.contains`'s page shows both.

**The sites.** Eighteen slices to the end of a `String` and ten of a `Bytes` read `drop`. The prefix and suffix sites of `tools/site` and `examples/fetch.ern` read `stripPrefix` and `stripSuffix`, four of them `String.splitOnce` matched against an empty first part. Two guards that choose a clause keep `startsWith` with `drop`, in `examples/fetch.ern` and `libs/markdown`, since a guard binds nothing. `libs/http`'s one `Bytes.endsWith` with a slice stays. `tools/site/links.ern`'s `Some(#("", _))` is a link with no path, not a prefix, and stays.
