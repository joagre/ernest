## The Reaper's Look at Rest, 2026-10-03

A deadlock is found by the reaper, which wakes after a tenth of a second without a message and looks (§8.6). A host has no notice to give that every process waits, and the runtime does not own the scheduler, so it looks. Fixing the load's sample of the reaper (*The Hardening Built*) showed the look from outside, and it had never been weighed. Measured on this machine, a program at rest for thirty seconds, in a timed `receive` or with an alarm pending alike:

| the look | CPU, of one core | wake-ups a second |
|---|---|---|
| every tenth of a second, as built | 1.1% | 12 |
| every second | 0.27% | 3 |
| none | 0 | 0 |
| every tenth, the host's schedulers told not to spin | 0.13% | 12 |

The look itself is a few table reads where something can still deliver. What costs is the wake-up: the host's schedulers spin for about a millisecond after each before they sleep again. So only how often the reaper wakes matters, and a host at rest wakes for nothing, which is what CLAUDE.md's cost rule holds Ernest to.

Three ways were weighed. The look by its cause: after a look that found nothing able to deliver but a process still running, the next comes in a tenth of a second, since that process may be about to wait; after one that found something able to deliver, a source, a timed wait, a foreign call, the next comes in a second, since a deadlock can begin only once that has ended. It is ten lines and no new state, a quarter of the cost, and a deadlock in a program long quiet is found within a second where it was a tenth. No look while something can deliver: the runtime counts what can deliver as it begins and ends, and tells the reaper when the count reaches nothing; a program at rest then wakes for nothing at all. It needs the count kept exact on four paths, a source, a timed wait, a foreign call and the end of a process that held any, a message at each fall to nothing held back to one a look, and the short look kept for what is not counted, a call a system process is still answering; a count that drifts is a deadlock never found. And the host's schedulers told not to spin, one flag of the launcher, which changes how every busy program is scheduled and is no fix of the look.

Decided with the user and built the same day (the plan's item 26 of MVP 2.99b): the look by its cause. It is the least that needs less, and the count can follow if a program's rest is ever what is measured. Measured after: 0.3% of a core and three wake-ups a second at rest, in a timed `receive` and with an alarm pending alike.

## `Address.ask` Is Taken Out, 2026-10-03

`Address.ask` entered the prelude on 2026-10-02 (*MVP 2.99b's Questions, One by One*, entry 79) and was built the next day. The same evening it was taken out again, decided with the user, the verdict left to the principles. They decide as follows.

**Principle 5 and §9.** The prelude holds what no function written over the prelude could provide. A helper provides an ask's result with `spawn`, `Address.call` and `send`: `spawn(fn() = send(me, wrap(Address.call(address, request, ms))))` delivers `wrap(Some(v))` or `wrap(None)`, once. What the helper does not give is the answer's order among the callee's other messages to the asker, which was the case made for the primitive. But `Address.call`, the language's own request and reply, does not give that order either: an answer a call returns has overtaken whatever the callee sent before it. And a program that needs the order has the form that gives it, a request that carries an address, answered by `send` and seen through `via` where the mailbox types differ (§6.5). So the primitive stood on a property the rest of request and reply does not have, and plain messages already give.

**Principle 2.** The ask was `call`'s request with `via`'s delivery: it overlapped both and had no job of its own. That `call` waits and `ask` does not was read as two jobs.
