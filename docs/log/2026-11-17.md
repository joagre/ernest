# The decisions log, 2026-11-17

## Twenty-Six Hosts Are the Cost, 2026-11-17

MVP 4.16's item 72, measured again on 2026-11-16 by an agent, a job at a time, and closed with the user that night with nothing built.

**Measured again.** On an idle machine, `make -s all` with nothing to do took 1.38 s and 18.3 s of processor time, the median of 22 runs. It starts 26 hosts, 14 for `ern build` and 12 for `ern doc --man`. *Twenty-Seven Hosts for Nothing* counted 27. The manual's program runs only where one of ern(1)'s prerequisites is newer than the page, so that count met a make with work. The session counted 26 in a run of its own and timed five makes at 1.33 to 1.60 s.

**A job at a time.** Alone, each job's host starts in 0.11 s and 0.5 s of processor time. A pages job's own check is 0.03 s. A build's is 0.04 s for a library of one module, 0.11 s for the standard library and 0.15 s for `tools`. *Twenty-Seven Hosts for Nothing* read each root's check as in the noise. That holds for the pages jobs alone.

**Where a build's check goes.** From one trace of each build, good to about a tenth. Every source is parsed, 46 to 78 ms for the standard library and for `tools`. Each source is hashed and its `.erc` read and compared, 31 to 51 ms. Naming the modules takes about 20 ms even for a library of one module. By the agent's reading that step reads the standard library's interfaces from all 27 of its `.erc` files, which it did not time apart. The standard library's hash reads them again, 12 to 17 ms. Each source is read twice, and each module's own `.erc` twice.

**One host, probed.** By the session, with a scratch module that runs the 26 jobs' command lines in turn through `ern_cli:ern/2` in one host, three runs each way, pinned to fewer threads by `taskset`. On 24 threads make took 1.35 to 1.43 s and the one host 0.52 s. On 8 threads they took 1.39 to 1.45 s and 0.46 s, on 4 threads 2.06 to 2.11 s and 0.46 s, and on 2 threads 3.41 to 3.45 s and 0.52 to 0.54 s. The 26 jobs take 0.41 s inside the one host, where their checks sum to about 1.4 s in hosts of their own, since the host loads its code once.

**On two old machines.** The same makes and the same probe, after a pull and a build. On xena, four cores of 2012, make with nothing to do took 5.5 to 5.6 s and the one host 1.6 to 1.7 s. On bee, two cores, they took 16.4 s and 3.4 to 3.5 s. A bare host starts in 0.32 s on xena and 0.55 s on bee. The standard library's build with nothing to do takes 0.64 s and 1.31 s, and built whole 3.0 s and 5.6 s.

**No stamp.** The plan's item had make start no job for a root whose sources have not changed. That is the stamp *Make Runs `ern build` Every Time* removed and *A Make With Nothing to Do* refused again: a second rule beside §11.1's that guesses at its answer (principle 2), and whose miss is silent (principle 3). The item cited neither entry, and make keeps no stamp.

**Who pays.** The item read the cost at the repository's own make. Only who builds Ernest from source pays that, and building Ernest may take its time. What a user pays is one `ern build`. So the question is whether that job is too slow, and the evening's measurements were made before it was asked.

**`ern build` is not slow.** With work it compiles in the order of the host's compiler. On bee the standard library built whole, 9,195 lines, took 5.7 s, about 1,600 lines a second. `erlc` over the repository's 52,586 lines of Erlang took 58 s there, about 900 a second. On nike the standard library takes 0.93 s. With nothing to do the job takes about two starts of the host. bee's whole make after a pull took 108 s with both cores busy, which is that machine's work and no defect.

**One host for make's jobs, dropped.** It would speed the building of Ernest and no user's build. An agent's build of it was stopped unfinished, and its edits were taken back.

**The leaner check, dropped.** A job with nothing to do ends in about a start (CLAUDE.md's *Design*), and a build takes two. The second is the check. Reading each file once would gain a few milliseconds, by the session's estimate. Leaving the parse out would gain about 50 ms of the standard library's 0.24 s. It touches the decision of what is current, where a miss is a stale build that says nothing (principle 3). *The Tools Measured* left the parse for a tenth of a second, and it is a tenth of a second still. A program whose build with nothing to do is felt brings the question back with its number.

**A compile server, dropped.** It would take two costs that stand. A build's work runs in one process, and every call pays its host's start and its code's loading. A host that compiles side by side and one that stays between calls are a server's two halves. *Twenty-Seven Hosts for Nothing* weighed the second and set it aside. Neither is built, and a need met in use brings each back on its own merits.

**What it cost.** The measuring agent took 21 minutes. The session's probes took a minute on nike, and 2 and 4 minutes on the old machines. The turn cost an evening: the plan's item named make's cost, and the work followed it there before anyone asked who pays it.

**The rule that came of it.** CLAUDE.md's rule for a step that looks wrong read as one for a step met while building, and an item that stands in the plan read as decided. A plan's item and the user's own idea are steps too: before work starts on one, the session says whether it is worth doing, and says no where it is not. The rule went to the template as `v0.10.12` and to both copies.

## The Busy Wait Stays, 2026-11-17

MVP 4.16's item 82, measured on 2026-11-16 by an agent and closed with the user that night. The variants are the host as `ern` starts it, the same with the schedulers' busy wait off, and, for a job that runs no program, one scheduler of each kind.

**A program that does nothing.** Under `ern run`, alone, it took 0.218 s as now and 0.227 s with the busy wait off, the same to within 8 ms, and 1.21 s of processor time against 0.33 s. Beside the flake hunt it took 1.22 s against 0.80 s. Beside 24 busy loops it took 8.6 s against 1.5 s.

**A build.** Its work runs in one process, as does that of every job that runs no program. The standard library's build with nothing to do, alone, took 0.244 s as now, 0.275 s with the busy wait off and 0.277 s with one scheduler. Built whole it took 0.931 s, 0.979 s and 1.025 s, in 2.45 s, 1.11 s and 1.18 s of processor time.

`make -s all` with nothing to do turns the other way: 1.44 s, 1.25 s and 1.14 s, in 18.8 s, 8.9 s and 7.6 s of processor time. The session timed five makes each way at 1.48 to 1.58 s, 1.27 to 1.32 s and 1.12 to 1.22 s.

**A running program.** `make bench` ran three times each way, in 446 s as now and 467 s with the busy wait off. A column counts as moved where every run one way lies beyond every run the other way, by more than the runs' own spread and by 3 %. Of 627 columns 31 moved, where chance gives about 11.

With the busy wait off the host's own call with one caller is 38 % slower and `Address.call` 14 to 16 %. A spawn's steps in the code table are 46 to 66 % slower, and a call to this node's process takes 2.0 µs where it took 1.6. The tables of bursts, spawns, monitors, restarts and the language's constructs did not move.

**Decided with the user: the busy wait stays.** The plan asked whether the launcher turns it off. Off, every call of a running program pays for a start, and what Ernest adds to a host operation costs a fraction of it (CLAUDE.md's *Design*).

**One scheduler for a job that runs no program, dropped.** Its work is one process's, the argument of *The First Start's Schedulers*. But alone the job is slower, a build by 19 ms with nothing to do and by 8 % with work, and one build alone is what a user runs. What it gained was among the hosts a make starts side by side, which is the building of Ernest itself (*Twenty-Six Hosts Are the Cost*).

**What is left as it is.** A job that runs a program starts in 8.6 s beside a load on every processor, a load no target runs. A second measurement, of a host that boots with one scheduler online, was stopped unfinished. Its number decided nothing: on a quiet machine a start takes the same either way.

**What it cost.** The first measurement took 1 h 30 min, 46 minutes of it six runs of the bench, where two short runs of the call table would have decided the busy wait. No brief stated the decision its numbers served or a bound in time, the mistake *Two Hours for a Hunt* records for a hunt.

## A Document Beside a Module That Fails, 2026-11-17

MVP 4.16's item 36, found on 2026-11-13 by item 26's build (*A Hiding Module Refused*) and built by an agent.

**Weighed first.** Before the work the item was read for whether it is worth doing. The report promised it in two places: §11.4 compiles the directory as `ern build` does, and §11.1 has a refused module fail that file alone in `ern doc` as in the build. A probe showed the code part from both. With one module broken and another changed, `ern doc` wrote nothing and the changed module kept its old document, where the build wrote the other module's `.erc`.

The narrower promise, that `ern doc` writes nothing where a module fails, was weighed and not taken. It would have taken `ern doc` out of §11.1's sentence and made the two directory jobs differ, in a section that follows the build throughout. A reader who knows `ern build` predicts the same of `ern doc` (principle 1).

**What `index.md` lists, decided with the user.** Each module of the directory whose document stands. A failed module's old document is left, as the build leaves its old `.erc`, and its line stays with it; on a first run it has neither. So a failing run changes nothing about the module that failed. Listing only the modules that compiled would leave an old document standing unlisted, and write the index at the failure and again at the fix.

**What was built.** `ern doc src-dir`, with `--man` or without, writes every document and page but those of a module that failed or that uses one that failed, and reports each failure as `ern build` does, with the status 1. The index is made from every source of the directory whose path names a module, since a module refused before it is compiled, one that hides a library's module among them, is not among the modules the build orders. A module is listed only where a regular file stands at its document's place. A `--man` run's index had followed a link there, against §11.4's rule for a link, and no longer does.

**The sweep's condition.** §11.4 had a run remove the documents of a module whose source is gone, "as the build removes its `.erc`", and gave no condition. The build sweeps after a successful compilation (§11.1), and until now a run with a failure wrote and removed nothing, so the question could not arise. The code kept the build's condition, and §11.4 now states it: a run in which no module failed removes them, and every run writes the index without such a module. A regression test holds it. It covers a module that fails its check, and not one refused before it is compiled.

## The Session's Own Suggestion, 2026-11-17

A clause added to CLAUDE.md's rule for a step that looks wrong, with the user, and carried to the template as `v0.10.13` and to both copies.

**What happened.** The session that wrote the sentence of *Twenty-Six Hosts Are the Cost* into the four files carried it there itself, on the stronger model. Told that an agent should have, it answered with two rule changes. Asked whether it recommended them, it kept half, then none, and then recommended this clause. Each was given as a recommendation, and only the last had been weighed before it was made.

**The rule.** A plan's item, the user's own idea and a suggestion of the session's own are steps too: before work starts on one, say whether it is worth doing, and say no where it is not.

**Why.** A recommendation is what a session is for, and one made unweighed costs the trust in the next. The rules already ask that what is brought to the user comes with a recommendation and its reason, and the unweighed ones came so in form. The sentence of the same day named a plan's item, and the same session weighed item 36 before it started it (*A Document Beside a Module That Fails*). It worked where it named the case, so it names this one. It makes the miss less likely and not impossible.

**Dropped.** Two rules were weighed and not made. One had a session read `docs/agents.md` before its first piece of work: that file holds nothing that decides who does the work. The other named the carry of a settled change to the template and the copies as an agent's: its saving was never measured, and *The repository* already has agents do the work. The carry of this clause went to an agent on the smallest model, from a brief holding every sentence, and took 83 seconds.

## A Termination Lost With OTP Alone, 2026-11-17

MVP 4.16's item 69, by one agent. *A Program Named Before It Runs* left OTP's part unreproduced. It is reproduced now with `erl`, `sh`, `cat` and `kill`, and `docs/otp_bugs.md`'s 22 is the report. Filing is the user's.

**The reproduction.** One `erl` opens a `cat`, takes its number from `port_info/2`, and has a long-lived `sh` send it the termination. It then writes to the `cat`, and one that echoes ran past the signal. On an idle machine of 24 threads, 20 runs of 1,000 rounds lost between 199 and 932. Pinned to one processor, 3 runs lost 11, 24 and 60. Beside 24 busy loops, 5 runs lost between 102 and 397. The session ran the report's block three times more, idle, and lost 354, 338 and 205.

**Lost, not late.** Each `cat` that lived on held no pending signal, no longer ignored the termination, and ended at a second one. Named once it had echoed, none of 9,000 lived on.

**Load is not needed.** The earlier entry has the window open under load. It shows most on an idle machine, since the forked process has often not run when `open_port/2` returns. Load makes it long enough for a slow sender: a `kill` through `os:cmd` lost none of 3,000 idle, and 3 and 13 of 1,000 beside the loops. A window as long as the start of `ern stop`'s host, which the test met, was not shown with OTP alone.

**The fix OTP could make.** The lines are the same at OTP-29.1.2 and at master, and the tracker holds no report of it. The forker already keeps `SIGCHLD` blocked across its fork. A copy of its pattern in C lost nearly every signal without a block of the termination, and none of 30,000 with it. POSIX leaves a signal that is blocked and ignored to the system, and Linux keeps it. The report says both.

**The three tests** that name a cat once it has echoed cite the report in their comments: `signal_jobs_take_any_mode_test`, `signaled_test` and `pid_lock`. No test changed.

**What is left as it is.** The idle runs spread from 199 to 932 under the same conditions. That is not explained, and no number of it decides anything.
