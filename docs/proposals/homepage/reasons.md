# ernest-lang.org: the reasons

Why [`proposal.md`](proposal.md) says what it says, and what was tried and set aside on the way. Nothing here is normative.

## The aim

To catch the reader in the first sixty seconds. The reader is from the BEAM, as the log's *The Front Page From Its Motive* has it.

The discussion began from Unison's page *The big idea*. That page opens with a mechanism and asks the reader to believe six benefits; its only code is `increment`. Ernest's homepage opens with an effect and puts a shell under it, where the visitor does it on two nodes.

## What the rehearsal's first screen held

On a laptop: the heading, the opening, the four claims and the top of the counter. That is some 170 words of prose, with the session's end and Try it below the fold.

*The Site's Look, by Hand* says the page's claim is one the visitor runs and not one they are told. The first screen was the telling.

## The homepage's changes

**The example straight under the opening.** Its heading, "A counter on another node", and its sentence both said the page's heading again. Without them the example stands 125 pixels higher, and the button comes onto the first screen. The claim was said five times before the code, and is said three times now.

**The space above the example.** With its heading gone, the example stood 20 pixels under the claims, and that was cramped. It is 32 now, the space the stylesheet gives above a section's heading, so the example reads as a section of its own without one.

**The opening.** Its first paragraph keeps to what the node is sent and what Ernest is. The sentence on programs written in Ernest from the start was a caveat that answers a question the visitor has not asked yet; Why has it.

Its second paragraph introduces the example, so that `foo` and the counter are met before the blocks. It says what Try it gives, in the announcement's words: a shell and a `foo` of the visitor's own.

It says what `foo` is before it names it: "a bare node beside it, `foo`". With the name first, a reader met `foo` before its kind, and took the words after the comma for something else.

"Nothing of the program", and not "nothing": the node needs Ernest itself installed.

The three pointers, to Why, the example and the tour, stood in one paragraph for a while. Each now stands where its reader is: Why at the first paragraph's end, the example's at the second's, the tour's under the example.

"Why Ernest is made so is the whole argument" was the page's most awkward sentence. "Why Ernest gives the whole argument" uses the document's own title as the link.

**Try it under the session, at the right.** The session is three lines shorter than the counter, so the button fits beside the counter's last lines and takes no row of its own.

**The counter's block with Copy alone.** The design note calls Try it "a block's Paste made prominent". While the Paste showed under the pointer alone, nobody saw both. Shown always, two buttons with one glyph did one thing, 250 pixels apart.

**The tour's sentence under the example.** It gives the announcement's second point in a form the claims do not have: the running counter's code updated, its state intact. After the example is where a reader asks what comes next.

**The first claim.** It said the heading again, word for word in its first half. The design note's rule is that each thing is said once on the page, the opening the effect and the list the means.

A first rewording gave the means alone: the hash, and a module's versions. Read from Why's side that was wrong: on Why's own page the claim "Code travels" no longer said that code travels. The sentence taken gives the means first and then both effects, "so it crosses to a node that lacks it, and a module keeps every version it has had". It serves both pages, and costs the homepage one line, 21 pixels.

**The mailbox's sentence.** "Which a function that receives names in its own type": a reader takes "a function that receives names" as one phrase first, and must read again. The README's sentence for the same claim is plainer and says what the programmer gains.

## The tools

**Always shown.** The design note already has an input's Paste "visible without a hover", and *The Site's Look, by Hand* gives the reason: a control shown only under a hover was found by nobody. That holds for a block's tools, and on every page.

**On every page and not the homepage alone.** A reader who met the tools on the homepage looks for them in the tour. A reader who lands on a guide's page from a search never passes the homepage.

**As small buttons, in green.** In the rehearsal's muted gray, always shown, they were lost. The stylesheet's brown was lost too: against a block's color it has 4.2 to 1, where the gray has 5.1 and the accent's green 5.8.

A toned-down red was seen well and not taken. It is a new color, which neither the stylesheet nor the logo has. It needs a value of its own in the dark scheme. It stands beside the button the design note calls the page's one strong color. And beside code a red reads as the mark of an error.

The green is the accent's, the color of a reserved word and of Try it. In a small button it stands out by its shape, without a new color. An input's Paste carries the glyph Try it carries, so the sign for putting code into the shell has one color and one shape everywhere.

**Above a first line that reaches them.** Always shown, the tools cover the end of a first line that reaches them, and moving the pointer away no longer brings it back. With the panel beside the page a block of the example is 362 pixels wide in a window of 1440, and the counter's first line ran under its Copy. That is the state Try it leaves a laptop in.

On a row above every block nothing is covered, at 33 pixels a block. Measured on the chosen page, the counter then ended at 801 where it ended at 768, and Try it at 785 where it ended at 752. Every one of 225 blocks would pay for the few whose first line is long.

A rule by the block's width, above a block narrower than 520 pixels, was the mock's. It left five blocks covered at the page's full width: one in Why, one in the tour's chapter 4, two in the guide's page 7 and one in the deployment guide. Its number was chosen and not derived. Breaking those five lines would change the documents to suit the tool.

So the script measures, which is the rule the page means, with no number in it. The homepage with the panel closed is as chosen, since the counter's first line is short.

**One rule on a touch screen too.** The rehearsal puts the tools on the row above wherever no mouse is reported. With the script measuring, that case is the rule's own: on a phone most first lines reach the tools. A block with a short first line gets them in the corner and is 33 pixels shorter. The browser the tests run reports no mouse, so with one rule it draws what a laptop draws.

## The first screen on typical screens

The aim is a homepage that looks good on the screens its readers have, and not at one size.

**The width is settled by the page's own.** From 1200 pixels wide the page is 1120 pixels wide and centered, with every edge at the same height. A wider screen adds margin.

**The height varies.** A page gets its screen after the system's scaling, and less the browser's and the system's bars. The bars were estimated and not measured: a screen of 1366×768 leaves a page about 650 pixels high, 1536×864 about 730, 1440×900 about 780 and 1920×1080 about 950. 1536×864 is a laptop of 1920×1080 at a scaling of 125 percent.

**The tighter spaces.** On a page of 730 the chosen page's button ended 22 pixels below the first screen. Three spaces gave 36: 32 pixels above the heading became 16, 32 above the example became 24, and a claim's padding went from 8 to 6.4. Try it then ends at 716. Above the example 20 pixels had been cramped, and 24 were not.

Wider claims gave nothing. The heading stands in the opening's column and took a third line, and the page grew by 28 pixels.

**A page of 650 is not chased.** Its first screen ends in the example. Bringing the session's answer onto it takes about a hundred pixels, which only the claims or a paragraph of the opening could give.

**Three sizes named.** The design note names sizes of the page, which are exact, and `make test-browser` holds each. The first screen's margin is thin: one more line in a claim is 21 pixels, and the button had 16 to spare before the spaces were tightened.

**A claim's code on one line.** The fourth claim broke `ern test` across two lines, each word in a box of its own. Kept on one line it costs the claim no line, measured at 960, 1024, 1200 and 1536 pixels wide.

**No line above a block, for now.** The example's blocks were felt to come unannounced, the session above all, which stands under the fourth claim. A line above each block costs about 31 pixels, which puts Try it 17 pixels below a page of 730. The button on the first screen weighed more. The proposal keeps the two other ways as a note.

## The example's columns

The example's blocks stood in two equal columns with a gap of 20 pixels, under an opening and claims whose columns are `1fr` and `1.15fr` with a gap of 48. So the blocks' inner edges stood at 751 and 771 under the texts' at 699 and 747. On one grid the edges are the same, and the tour's sentence ends where the counter does.

The counter's block is 498 pixels wide where it was 550.

**One under the other in a narrower window.** The counter needs 481 pixels and the session 533, more than a window of 960 has for the two side by side, however it is split. Equal columns only move the scrolling from the counter to the session. What the shared columns cut at 960 is the end of the counter's signature, `receiving CounterMsg =`, which the second claim is about. A visitor scrolls down by habit and sideways rarely, and cut code reads as broken.

**Try it under the counter there.** The button is to be on the first screen always, which is why the page first chosen did not stack. With the session between the counter and the button, Try it ended at 1130 on a page of 950. Under the counter it ends at 854, and the session follows it.

**The tour's sentence under the counter.** It names "this counter", so it stands with the counter, in the counter's column, and needs no width of its own. In the narrower page Try it takes the place under the counter, and the sentence stands last, where a reader asks what comes next. Above the counter, it spoke of what comes next before the reader had seen the counter. Side by side it also moved the counter 61 pixels down, off a page of 730, with the session's top no longer level with the counter's.

## The header beside the panel

With the panel beside the page the header spans the window and the page keeps the left column. The rehearsal centers the header's content in the window, so the name stood at 160 pixels and the page's left edge at 24.

The rule holds only while the panel is beside. With the panel closed or below, the header and the page are centered alike, and a header at the edges would stand 136 pixels left of the page in that window.

## The laptop first

The page's one action is the shell, which wants a keyboard. A phone's visitor reads the claim and the example, and runs it later. So the first screen is designed for the laptop, and a phone gets no page of its own yet.

## The shell's editing

It was found by trying whether a visitor can change the counter that Try it puts into the shell.

The page says that `foo` holds none of the counter's code. The session shows a spawn, a send and `Some(5)`, which is what an Erlang reader expects of `spawn(Node, Fun)` on a node that has the module. Nothing printed tells the two cases apart.

An absence cannot be shown. A narrower promise can: `foo` runs what the visitor types. Code typed a moment ago in the visitor's own browser cannot have been installed on `foo` beforehand. A visitor who makes the counter count by twos and gets `Some(10)` from `foo` has shown themselves what "bare" means.

That needs the counter to be edited in the shell. It can be, and no visitor would: no key moves between the lines of an input. A first try assumed that the up arrow moves up a line; the edit landed before the last brace, and the input was refused with a type error. A visitor with history would see the counter vanish at that key.

**The argument for the arrows** is principle 1: a reader whose cursor stands in a block of lines predicts that up goes up a line. Readline has no input of several lines, so its rule for the arrows was not made for this case. fish and IPython move within the input and walk the history from its first and last line.

**For the five things to decide:**

1. §11.2 has `C-p` and `C-n` as the arrows' keys today, and in Emacs `C-p` is the line above.
2. Once the arrows move by line, a `C-a` that leaves the line would surprise.
3. The report is silent on the column, and a user meets it.
4. Walking the history should take one press an entry. fish's way, where the arrows keep walking until another key is pressed, is a mode the screen does not show.
5. The arrows already stand beside `C-b` and `C-f`, and `Backspace` beside `C-h`. A visitor's keyboard has `Home`, `End` and `Delete`.

Two searches of the log found no entry that weighed the arrows in an input of several lines. For the three keys they found one entry, of 2026-09-30, which stopped an unbound key from typing its escape sequence and did not weigh binding it.

## Tried and set aside

**Another layout.** It put the example beside the heading and the claims under the button. Over eight pages of the mock it gained the counter above the session, a small text above each block, an invitation under the session and an opening that set the scene. Set beside the rehearsal at the end, the rehearsal's layout was liked better. What it taught was kept:

- A name is introduced before it is used: the counter's code before the session that names `count`, and `foo` before the example.
- A sentence set as a caption, small and muted above a block, was not seen.
- A text is read where the reader can follow it.

**A module's versions in the opening.** "A module keeps every version it has had: a new one loads without disturbing the processes that run older ones." The first claim beside it says the same, and nothing on the page shows a module's versions. *The Front Page From Its Motive* placed the versions in the first claim's sentence.

**Running without OTP's behaviors, in the opening.** It says what Ernest is without, on the first screen.

**"Another Ernest node" in the heading.** It took a third line on a laptop and a fourth on a phone, and on Ernest's own site the node can be no other.

**A listing of what `foo` holds.** The visitor would have to trust that the list is whole and that the counter is missing from it. Ernest has no function that asks a node what it holds, and none is proposed.

**A line from the shell saying what crossed at a spawn.** Every operator would read it at every first spawn, for the sake of one page.

**Another example in place of the counter**, one line long, so that no editing is needed. It would hide the shell's finding and not answer it.

## A finding withdrawn

On a phone the session's first input is wider than the screen, and its Paste stands over the input's text. That was reported as a defect. The design note has it as the rule: an input's Paste "is held at the block's visible right edge where the input is longer than the block is wide", and the line scrolls under it.

## How the figures were made

Every render was made in a headless Chromium, which reports no mouse. Left alone it draws a block as a touch screen has it, with its tools on a row above, 32 pixels high. The first figures of the discussion were of that layout and were corrected. The figures in `proposal.md` were made with the mouse's rules forced, but for the phone's.
